# What Is a Consent Agenda? How Boards Use One to Win Back Meeting Time

## A plain guide to the consent agenda for board meetings, with a consent agenda sample you can copy

A consent agenda is a group of routine board items that are approved together, in one motion and one vote, without discussion. Typical items are the previous minutes, reports that need no action and routine approvals. Any board member can ask to move an item off the consent agenda so it can be discussed and voted on separately.

Boards use consent agendas to stop spending the first half hour of every meeting on paperwork. Done well, a consent agenda frees time for strategy. Done badly, it hides things the board should have looked at. This guide covers what belongs on one, what should never go on one, how the vote works, and a sample you can adapt.

## Why boards use a consent agenda

Most board meetings carry a pile of items that need a formal vote but no real debate: approving minutes, accepting committee reports, confirming dates. Voting on each one separately takes time, and it adds up. Boards that switch to a consent agenda often get back 15 to 30 minutes per meeting.

The time is not the only benefit:

- **It pushes reading before the meeting.** Directors have to read consent items in the board packet, because there is no presentation in the room.
- **It moves strategy up the agenda.** The time saved goes to the questions that actually need the board.
- **It makes the board look at what matters.** When routine items are grouped, the items left for discussion stand out.

## What goes on a consent agenda?

A consent agenda should only hold items that are routine, non-controversial and already understood by the board. Common examples:

- **Minutes** of the previous board meeting.
- **Committee reports** that are for information only.
- **Staff or officer reports** that need no decision.
- **Routine contracts** or renewals that are within an approved budget.
- **Confirmation of decisions** the board already discussed, such as dates for the next meetings.
- **Routine appointments,** such as confirming a committee member.
- **Correspondence** the board should note but not act on.

If you are not sure whether an item belongs, ask one question: would any director reasonably want to discuss this? If yes, it does not belong.

## What should not be on a consent agenda

Some items should always get their own discussion and vote. Keep these off the consent agenda:

- **The annual budget** and any major change to it.
- **The audit** and financial statements that need board approval.
- **Executive pay** and the CEO or executive director's evaluation.
- **New policies** or significant changes to existing ones.
- **Anything involving a conflict of interest.**
- **Large or unusual contracts,** or anything outside the approved budget.
- **Items the board has not seen before** and that need explanation.

The rule of thumb: if a decision carries legal, financial or reputational risk, it needs the board's full attention, not a bundled vote.

## How a consent agenda vote works

The process is short. Under *Robert's Rules of Order* and most board practice, it runs like this:

1. **The chair presents the consent agenda.** "The consent agenda includes the minutes of the September meeting, the finance committee report and the facilities contract renewal."
2. **The chair asks whether anyone wants to remove an item.** "Does any member wish to remove an item?"
3. **Any member can ask for an item to be removed.** No reason, second or vote is needed. The item moves to the regular agenda for discussion.
4. **The chair asks for a motion** to approve the remaining consent items.
5. **The board votes once** on the whole group.

The motion to approve a consent agenda often sounds like this: "I move that we approve the consent agenda as presented." If an item was removed: "...as amended, with the facilities contract moved to new business."

That removal right is what makes the system fair. A consent agenda is only acceptable to a board if every director knows they can pull any item at any time.

## A consent agenda sample you can copy

Here is a consent agenda sample, written the way it would appear on a board agenda. Adapt the items to your board.

> **5. Consent agenda (vote)**
>
> The following items are routine and will be approved in one motion unless a director asks for an item to be removed for discussion. Supporting documents are in the board packet.
>
> 5.1 Minutes of the [date] board meeting
> 5.2 Finance committee report, [month] (information only)
> 5.3 Governance committee report, [month] (information only)
> 5.4 Executive director's report, [month] (information only)
> 5.5 Renewal of the [service] contract, within the approved budget
> 5.6 Confirmation of [name] as a member of the [committee] committee
> 5.7 Board meeting dates for [year]: [dates]
>
> **Suggested motion:** "I move that we approve the consent agenda as presented."

Put the consent agenda early in the meeting, usually just after approving the agenda. That way, any item that gets pulled has time to be discussed properly.

## How to record a consent agenda in the minutes

The minutes do not need to repeat each item. They need to show 3 things:

- **What was on the consent agenda,** listed by item.
- **Any items removed,** and where they were moved.
- **The motion and the result,** including who moved and seconded it if your board records that.

For example: "The consent agenda, consisting of items 5.1 to 5.7, was approved as presented. Moved by [name], seconded by [name]. Carried."

If an item was removed, record its discussion and vote under the agenda item it moved to.

## Common consent agenda mistakes

- **Late board packets.** If directors get the documents the night before, they cannot read them, and the consent agenda becomes a rubber stamp.
- **Hiding decisions.** Slipping a contested item into the consent agenda to avoid debate destroys trust fast.
- **Pressuring people not to pull items.** Removing an item should never feel like an accusation.
- **No supporting documents.** Every consent item should have a document in the packet, even if it is one page.
- **Forgetting to explain it.** New directors need to be told how the consent agenda works and that they can pull any item.

## How to make consent agendas easier to run

A consent agenda depends on two things: a board packet that goes out early, and minutes that are accurate. Both are easier with a few habits:

- **Send the packet 5 to 7 days ahead,** with consent items clearly marked.
- **Keep committee reports short** and end each one with "information only" or "action requested".
- **Write the minutes the same day,** while the motions are fresh.

## Consent agenda checklist

- Only routine, non-controversial items that the board already understands.
- No budgets, audits, executive pay, new policies or conflicts of interest.
- Every item backed by a document in the board packet.
- Packet out 5 to 7 days before the meeting.
- The chair asks whether anyone wants to remove an item before the vote.
- One motion, one vote for everything that remains.
- Minutes list the items, any removals and the result.

A consent agenda works when directors trust that they can always pull an item, and know they have read everything they did not.
