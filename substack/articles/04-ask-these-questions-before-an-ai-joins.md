# Ask these 7 questions before an AI joins your meeting

*AI in meetings only works if people trust it. Four etiquette rules, and what to ask a tool before it listens.*

---

You've probably been on this call.

You join a few seconds early. The participant list already has four names on it: three of them are AI notetakers, each sent by a different person, none of them human. Someone jokes, "Should we just let the bots talk to each other?" Everyone laughs. Nobody turns them off.

AI in meetings went from novelty to default very quickly, and the norms haven't caught up. Bloomberg ran a column with the headline [Please Stop Inviting AI Notetakers to Meetings](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-14/ai-notetakers-in-meetings-are-making-me-queasy). Legal teams ask people to keep bots out of sensitive calls, and some companies now remove unapproved notetakers from meetings by policy.

We make an AI meeting assistant, so we have an obvious interest here. That's exactly why we think this deserves a straight answer: **if the people in the meeting don't trust the AI in it, the notes don't matter.**

## Why trust is the whole game

People speak differently when they know they're being recorded. They hedge. They save the real objection for the hallway, or for a private message after the call. The meeting gets more polite and less useful.

So an AI that records everything but makes the room guarded can leave you with a perfect transcript of a worse conversation.

The fix is mostly manners, and a few hard questions to whoever makes the tool.

## The etiquette: four rules for bringing AI into a meeting

### 1. Ask first, every time there's someone new

A simple "Are you okay if I use an AI assistant to take notes on this call?" at the start. If anyone hesitates, turn it off. Hesitation *is* the answer.

### 2. Say what it's for

"It's so I can focus on the conversation instead of typing, and so I can send you an accurate recap." People are far more comfortable when they know the purpose and who will see the output.

### 3. One assistant per meeting

If three people bring three bots, agree on one and turn off the rest. Then share its notes with everyone, so nobody feels they need their own.

### 4. Share the output with the room

The fastest way to build trust is to send everyone the same notes. A recording only one side can see feels like surveillance. A recap everyone receives feels like a service.

## In-person meetings need extra care

Online, the platform at least shows a "recording" banner or a bot in the participant list. In a conference room, a café, or a site visit, there is no banner. The phone on the table looks like a phone.

That makes the verbal "ask first" more important, not less. Say it out loud at the start, and again if someone joins halfway through.

It also matters legally. Rules about recording conversations differ by country and, in the US, by state: some places require the consent of everyone in the conversation, not just one person. And under GDPR, a recording or transcript that identifies people is personal data, so the lawful basis for capturing it should be settled before you hit record ([a practical overview here](https://www.spinach.ai/blog/gdpr-ai-meeting-tools-guide)). This isn't legal advice. It's a reason to ask your legal team once and write down the answer.

> In a room with no recording banner, your voice is the banner. Say it out loud.

## The 7 questions to ask any AI meeting tool

Manners handle the people. These questions handle the tool. Ask them before your team adopts anything, and expect specific answers, not "we take security seriously."

### 1. Where is our data processed, and can we choose the region?

Not just where it's *stored*. Where the AI models that transcribe and summarize it actually run. For teams in regulated industries or regions with data-residency rules, this is often the deciding question.

### 2. Is our data used to train models?

Yours, or any third-party model provider's. Get the answer in writing, including for sub-processors.

### 3. Who can see recordings and transcripts by default?

Everyone on the invite? The whole company? Only the person who recorded? Defaults matter more than settings, because most people never change them.

### 4. How long is it kept, and can we delete it?

Look for clear retention controls and real deletion, not just hiding it from the interface.

### 5. How do participants know it's there?

Is the assistant visible in the meeting? Does it announce itself? Can any participant ask for it to stop?

### 6. Does it work where our meetings actually happen?

In-person conversations, multiple languages, patchy connectivity on the road. A tool that only works for well-connected video calls in one language will miss a lot of the meetings that matter.

### 7. What certifications and agreements back this up?

Security audits, a data processing agreement, the list of sub-processors. Ask for the documents, not the badge on the website.

If a vendor can't answer one of these clearly, that's your answer.
