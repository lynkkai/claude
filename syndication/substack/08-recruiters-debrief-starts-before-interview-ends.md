# Recruiters: The Interview Debrief Starts Before the Interview Ends

*Most hiring mistakes aren't made in the interview. They're made in the debrief, from memory, three days later.*

Welcome back to **After the Call**. This week's issue is for recruiters, hiring managers and anyone who has sat in a debrief thinking, "wait, which candidate said that?"

## The debrief problem

Picture a typical hiring loop. A candidate meets four interviewers over a day. Each interviewer has three other loops that week. The debrief happens on Thursday.

By Thursday, here's what each interviewer remembers: a general impression, one or two vivid moments, and the scorecard they filled in (if they filled it in right away, which many didn't).

The debrief then becomes a contest of impressions. The most confident voice in the room wins. Specific evidence, like what the candidate actually said about their last project, is mostly gone.

That's how good candidates get rejected for "not seeming senior enough" and weaker ones get hired because they were memorable.

## Evidence beats impressions

Structured interviewing research has argued for years that consistent questions and evidence-based scoring predict job performance better than unstructured conversations and gut feel. The practical side of that is simple: **write down what the candidate said, not what you thought of it.**

"Strong on system design" is an impression.
"Described splitting a monolith into three services; explained the data migration plan and the rollback strategy" is evidence.

Evidence is what makes a debrief fair, and it's what lets an interviewer who disagrees point at something concrete.

## Three ways to capture evidence

**1. Take verbatim notes during the interview.** Effective but exhausting, and it splits your attention. Candidates notice when you're typing more than listening.

**2. Write a structured scorecard right after.** Ten minutes, immediately, before the next meeting. Works well if interviewers actually do it, and it's the minimum every team should require.

**3. Record and transcribe (with consent).** The interviewer listens fully, and the transcript provides the evidence. The scorecard is then written from the transcript, quoting the candidate's words.

Option 3 is increasingly common. If you go that way, a few rules:

- **Tell candidates in advance** that interviews are recorded, explain why (fairness and accuracy), and offer a way to opt out without penalty.
- **Check local law.** Recording consent rules vary by country and state.
- **Limit access** to the hiring team, and set a retention period. Don't keep recordings of rejected candidates forever.

If you have existing recordings to transcribe, here's a general guide to [transcribing audio to text](https://lynkk.ai/blog/transcribe-audio-to-text), from free built-in options to paid tools.

## A better debrief format

Whatever capture method you use, the debrief itself improves with structure:

1. **Scorecards submitted before the debrief**, so nobody anchors on the first opinion voiced.
2. **Each interviewer shares evidence, then a score**, in that order.
3. **Disagreements are resolved by evidence**: "What did they say that made you rate them a 2?"
4. **The decision and its reasons are written down**, so the next loop for the same role can learn from it.

A consistent written summary for each candidate (strengths with evidence, concerns with evidence, decision, next step) works much like any good [meeting summary](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-summary-template). The structure is the point.

## This week's takeaway

The debrief is only as good as the evidence that reaches it. Capture what candidates said, while they're saying it or immediately after, and your hiring decisions get fairer and faster.
