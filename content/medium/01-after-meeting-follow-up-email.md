# How to Write a Follow-Up Email After a Meeting (7 Samples You Can Copy)

## Short templates for clients, teams, sales calls and interviews, plus how to send a recap with meeting notes and action items

A good follow-up email after a meeting does three things: it thanks people for their time, it confirms what was decided, and it makes it obvious who is doing what by when. That's it. Send it within 24 hours, keep it short enough to read on a phone, and put the action items where nobody can miss them.

Below you'll find a simple structure that works for almost any meeting, a list of subject lines, and seven meeting follow-up email samples you can copy and adapt.

## Why the follow-up email matters more than the meeting

Every meeting ends with a feeling of agreement. Everyone nodded. Everyone "got it." Then a day passes, and three people remember three different versions of what was decided.

The follow-up email is how a conversation becomes a commitment. It gives everyone a written record, it catches misunderstandings while they're still cheap to fix, and it quietly shows that you were paying attention.

For client and sales meetings it does one more thing: it keeps momentum. A prospect who gets a clear recap the same afternoon has an easy next step. A prospect who hears nothing for a week has already moved on to something else.

## The 4 parts of a good meeting follow-up email

Almost every strong follow-up email after a meeting has the same four parts.

**1. A quick thank-you.** One line. "Thanks for the time today" is enough. Skip the paragraph of pleasantries.

**2. A short summary.** Two or three bullets covering what was discussed and, more importantly, what was decided. If there was a decision, write it as a decision ("We'll launch on the 14th"), not as a topic ("We discussed the launch date").

**3. Action items with owners and dates.** This is the part people actually come back to. Every item needs a verb, an owner and a due date. "Send pricing" is vague. "Priya sends updated pricing by Thursday" is an action item.

**4. One next step.** End with exactly one clear next step: the next meeting, a decision you need, or a document you're waiting on. If you need a reply, ask a direct question.

Optional extras: a link to the full meeting notes, the recording, or any documents that came up.

## When to send it

Same day is ideal. Within 24 hours is the rule. After 48 hours the details blur, and the email starts to feel like an afterthought instead of a recap.

If you can't write the full recap right away, send a two-line note ("Thanks for today. Full notes and next steps coming tomorrow morning.") so people know it's on the way.

## Subject lines that get opened

Make the subject line say what the email is. People search their inbox for these later, so clarity beats cleverness.

- Recap and next steps: [Meeting name], [Date]
- Follow-up from today's call: action items inside
- Notes from our [Project] meeting on [Date]
- Great speaking today, [Name]: next steps
- [Their company] + [Your company]: summary and timeline
- Action needed by Friday: [Project] decisions

## 7 follow-up email samples you can copy

Replace anything in [brackets]. Delete any line that doesn't apply. Shorter is better.

### 1. After a client meeting

> **Subject:** Recap and next steps: [Project] review, [Date]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for the time today. Here's a quick recap so we're aligned.
>
> **What we agreed**
> - [Decision 1]
> - [Decision 2]
>
> **Action items**
> - [Your name]: [task] by [date]
> - [Client name]: [task] by [date]
>
> **Next step:** [Next meeting or deliverable] on [date]. Let me know if I missed anything.
>
> Best,
> [Your name]

### 2. After an internal team meeting

> **Subject:** [Team] sync, [Date]: decisions and owners
>
> Hi all,
>
> Quick summary of today's sync.
>
> **Decisions**
> - [Decision]
> - [Decision]
>
> **Action items**
> - [Name]: [task] by [date]
> - [Name]: [task] by [date]
> - [Name]: [task] by [date]
>
> **Still open:** [Question nobody could answer yet]. [Name] will find out before our next sync on [date].
>
> Full notes are here: [link]
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### 3. After a sales discovery call

> **Subject:** Great speaking today, [Name]: next steps
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for walking me through how [their company] handles [process] today. It sounds like the biggest priorities are:
>
> 1. [Pain point, in their words]
> 2. [Pain point, in their words]
> 3. [Goal or deadline they mentioned]
>
> As promised, I've attached [resource]. On my side, I'll [your action] by [date].
>
> Would [day] at [time] work for a 30-minute demo with [other stakeholder]? I'll tailor it to the points above.
>
> Best,
> [Your name]

Tip: repeat the prospect's problems in their own words. It proves you listened, and it makes your email easy for them to forward internally.

### 4. Sharing meeting notes and action items

Use this one when you're the designated note-taker, or when people who missed the meeting need to catch up.

> **Subject:** Notes from [Meeting name], [Date]
>
> Hi everyone,
>
> Here are the notes from today's meeting. Attendees: [names].
>
> **Summary**
> [Two or three sentences on what the meeting was about and how it ended.]
>
> **Decisions**
> - [Decision]
> - [Decision]
>
> **Action items**
> - [Task]: [Owner], due [date]
> - [Task]: [Owner], due [date]
> - [Task]: [Owner], due [date]
>
> **Next meeting:** [Date and time]
>
> Reply here if anything is wrong or missing and I'll update the notes.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### 5. After a networking or coffee meeting

> **Subject:** Great to meet you, [Name]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks again for making time for coffee today. I really enjoyed hearing about [specific thing they said].
>
> As promised, here's [the article, intro or resource we discussed]: [link].
>
> If there's ever anything I can help with on [their area], just let me know. I'd love to stay in touch.
>
> Best,
> [Your name]

Keep this one light. No list of action items and no ask beyond staying in touch, unless you agreed on something specific.

### 6. After a job interview

> **Subject:** Thank you: [Role] interview
>
> Hi [Interviewer name],
>
> Thank you for taking the time to speak with me today about the [Role] position. I enjoyed learning about [specific project or challenge they mentioned].
>
> Our conversation made me even more excited about the role, especially [specific detail]. My experience with [relevant skill] would help me [concrete contribution].
>
> Please let me know if there's anything else I can share. I look forward to hearing about next steps.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your name]

Send it within a few hours. If you met several interviewers, send each one a separate note with a different specific detail.

### 7. After a board or committee meeting

> **Subject:** [Committee] meeting, [Date]: summary and action items
>
> Dear members,
>
> Thank you for attending today's [committee] meeting. A summary is below. Formal minutes will follow for approval at our next meeting on [date].
>
> **Motions and decisions**
> - [Motion]: [passed / not passed]
> - [Decision]
>
> **Action items**
> - [Name]: [task] by [date]
> - [Name]: [task] by [date]
>
> **Carried to next meeting**
> - [Item]
>
> Please reply by [date] with any corrections.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Your name]
> [Title]

This is a summary, not the minutes. If your organization requires formal minutes, keep them as a separate document.

## Common follow-up email mistakes

- **Writing a transcript.** Nobody wants a play-by-play. Summarize outcomes, not conversation.
- **Action items with no owner.** "We'll look into it" means nobody will. Put a name on every task.
- **No dates.** "Soon" is not a deadline.
- **Burying the ask.** If you need a reply or a decision, put it near the top or in bold.
- **Sending it three days later.** By then the meeting feels old, and your recap has to compete with everyone's memory of it.
- **Copying everyone.** Send it to the people who were there and the people who own an action item. That's usually enough.

## How to write follow-up emails faster

The slowest part of a follow-up email isn't the writing. It's reconstructing what happened: scrolling through scribbled notes and trying to remember who agreed to what.

A few habits make that much easier:

- **Take notes in the shape of the email.** Keep three headings open during the meeting: Decisions, Action items, Next step. The recap is then mostly copy and paste.
- **Read the action items back before the meeting ends.** "So, to recap: Priya has pricing by Thursday, and I'll send the draft Monday." Thirty seconds of recap saves a round of corrections later.
- **Save your own templates.** Turn the samples above into email snippets or saved replies so you never start from a blank page.
- **Use an AI meeting assistant.** Tools like [Lynkk](https://lynkk.ai) record the meeting, online or in person, and within about 30 seconds of it ending give you structured notes and assigned action items. Your follow-up becomes an edit instead of a writing task. Always read it before you hit send, because you're still the one accountable for what it says. (Full disclosure: I work on Lynkk.)

## A quick checklist before you hit send

- Sent within 24 hours
- Clear subject line with the meeting name and date
- A one-line thank-you
- Decisions written as decisions
- An owner and a date on every action item
- One clear next step or question
- Short enough to read on a phone

A follow-up email after a meeting takes five minutes to write, and a week later it's often the only part of the meeting anyone remembers. Make it count.
