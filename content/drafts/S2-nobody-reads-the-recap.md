# Nobody Reads the Recap

*By [name], [role] at Lynkk*

---

We solved meeting capture and called it meeting productivity.

Think about what has actually changed in the last three years. Before, you came out of a meeting
with nothing, or with half a page of your own handwriting. Now you come out with a well-structured
summary, bullet points, decisions, and a list of action items with owners, delivered to your inbox
in under a minute.

Now ask the harder question. Did the follow-through get better?

For most teams I have seen, no. We replaced the unread handwritten note with an unread AI summary,
at considerable expense, and declared the problem solved because the artefact got better looking.

## The recap is an output, and the work needed an input

Here is the whole problem in one sentence: a summary is a record of what happened, and
follow-through requires something to happen next.

Those are different operations. The first is retrospective and the second is prospective, and
building a very good tool for the first one does nothing for the second. In fact there is a decent
argument it makes things slightly worse, because the existence of a tidy record creates the feeling
that the loop has been closed. It has not. It has been documented.

Watch what actually happens to a recap. It arrives. Someone glances at it on their phone between
two other calls. It contains four action items, two of which belong to other people and one of
which is a paraphrase of something they already know they need to do. None of them enter any system
where work is actually tracked. By Thursday it is below the fold in a thread.

The information was captured perfectly. Nothing moved.

## The three failures hiding inside "action items"

**Owners that are not people.** An action item that says "the team to follow up on pricing" will
never be done, and everybody reading it knows that instantly. Unassigned work is a note about
work.

**No date, or a date that means nothing.** "Soon", "next week", "after the review". A commitment
without a date is a sentiment.

**No destination.** This is the big one. An action item that lives only in the recap lives in a
document nobody opens. It has to arrive where the person already works: the issue tracker, the CRM
task list, the thing they look at on Monday morning. Capture that does not end in someone's actual
queue is capture that ends nowhere.

Notice that none of those three are capture failures. The AI heard the commitment correctly. The
system just stopped one step too early, at the point where the information became a document
instead of a task.

## The commitments that matter are the ones you made

There is an asymmetry worth naming. Recaps are organised around the meeting: here is what this
group discussed and decided.

But almost nobody's actual problem is "what did that meeting decide". Their problem is "what did I
promise, to whom, and have I done it". That list runs across every meeting they had, not within
any one of them, which means the recap format cannot represent it. You get fifteen recaps, each
containing one of your promises, and no list of your promises.

For anyone customer-facing, that list is the single most valuable thing in their working week, and
the most expensive thing to get wrong. The sentences beginning "I'll send you" are remembered
indefinitely by the customer and forgotten within hours by the person who said them. Not because
they are careless. Because they said it out loud in the middle of a conversation about something
else, and nothing wrote it down anywhere they will look.

## What would actually help

Less recap, more plumbing. Concretely:

**Commitments extracted across meetings, not within them.** One list, per person, of every promise
they made, with the moment they made it. That is a different query than "summarise this call", and
it is the one people need.

**Write to the system of record, not to a document.** The test of a meeting tool should be whether
anything appeared in the issue tracker or the CRM task list afterwards. If the only artefact is a
summary, the loop is open.

**The draft, not the reminder.** Telling someone they owe a follow-up email is a nag. Having the
follow-up email drafted, with the numbers and the promises from the call already in it, is help.
The difference between those two is most of the actual value.

**A human in the loop at the point of sending.** I would argue against automating the send. The
moment a system sends things to customers on your behalf, you have to read everything it sends
anyway, and now you are reviewing rather than writing, which is the worse job. Draft automatically,
send deliberately.

## The bit that is not a tooling problem

Having said all that: a meaningful share of this is not solvable by anybody's software.

If your team has back-to-back calls from nine to six, no amount of automated follow-through fixes
it, because there is no hour in which follow-through could happen. The recap is not the bottleneck.
The calendar is. Tools that promise to make meeting overload survivable are, at best, treating a
symptom, and we should be honest that we are selling into a problem partly created by the fact that
meetings are too cheap to schedule.

The most effective meeting intervention I know of remains deleting the meeting. No vendor is going
to tell you that, including this one, which is a reason to be slightly suspicious of all of us.

## Where this lands

Capture is a solved problem. Follow-through is not, and it never was a capture problem. It is a
question of whether the thing someone said out loud ever reaches the place where their work
actually lives.

Stop measuring whether the summary was good. Measure whether anything happened.
