# A Board Meeting Agenda Template US Boards Can Adopt and Reuse Through 2026

*By [Samarth Wagh](https://lynkk.ai/blog/authors/samarth-wagh), Co-founder, Lynkk.ai · Last updated 17 October 2026*

> **Quick answer:** A standard board meeting agenda follows this order: **(1) call to order, (2) roll call and quorum, (3) approval of the agenda, (4) approval of prior minutes, (5) consent agenda, (6) reports, (7) old business, (8) new business, (9) executive session if needed, (10) announcements and (11) adjournment.** The board chair and CEO or executive director prepare it and send it with the board packet about **5 to 7 days** before the meeting.

*Disclosure: Lynkk publishes this guide and makes an AI meeting assistant. This is general guidance; your bylaws and state law come first.*

A board meeting agenda is the written order of business for a board meeting: the items, the order they are taken in, who leads each one, and what the board is being asked to decide. A good one guards the board's time for decisions and strategy, keeps routine approvals fast, and leaves a clear trail for the minutes. A weak one lets reports eat the clock and pushes real decisions to "next time". Big difference.

Below: the standard order of business, then copy-ready templates for nonprofit and startup boards. Each sample board meeting agenda here can be pasted straight into your packet, and the practical rules most US boards apply follow them.

## What should be included in a board meeting agenda?

A board meeting agenda should include 11 standard items, in roughly this order. Many US boards base it on Robert's Rules of Order, adjusted by their bylaws.

| # | Agenda item | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Call to order | Chair opens the meeting, notes the time |
| 2 | Roll call and quorum | Record attendance, confirm quorum |
| 3 | Approval of the agenda | Board agrees on the order of business |
| 4 | Approval of prior minutes | Correct and approve last meeting's minutes |
| 5 | Consent agenda | Approve routine items in one vote |
| 6 | Reports | CEO or ED, treasurer, committees |
| 7 | Old (unfinished) business | Items carried over |
| 8 | New business | New items needing discussion or a vote |
| 9 | Executive session | Confidential matters, if needed |
| 10 | Announcements | Dates, events, reminders |
| 11 | Adjournment | Chair closes the meeting, notes the time |

## What does a board meeting agenda template look like?

Use this board meeting agenda format for any US board. Add times; that keeps reports from crowding out decisions.

```
[ORGANIZATION NAME]
BOARD OF DIRECTORS MEETING AGENDA
Date: [Thursday, November 13, 2026]   Time: [6:00 to 7:30 pm ET]
Location: [Address / video link]

1. Call to order (6:00) | [Board Chair]
2. Roll call and confirmation of quorum (6:02) | [Secretary]
3. Approval of the agenda (6:05)
4. Approval of minutes of [prior meeting date] (6:07)
5. Consent agenda (6:10)
   a. [Committee report]
   b. [Routine policy renewal]
6. Reports (6:15)
   a. CEO / Executive Director report | [Name]
   b. Treasurer's report and financial statements | [Name]
   c. Committee reports | [Chairs]
7. Old business (6:40)
   a. [Item] | Decision needed: [ ]
8. New business (6:55)
   a. [Item] | Decision needed: [ ]
9. Executive session, if needed (7:15)
10. Announcements (7:25)
11. Adjournment (7:30)

Board packet: [link]   RSVP by: [date]
```

## Why does a board meeting agenda matter?

Boards do not meet often. Only about a third of nonprofit boards meet more than 10 times a year, down from nearly half in 1994, according to [BoardSource's review of 21 years of governance data](https://blog.boardsource.org/blog/leading-with-intent-comes-of-age-a-glimpse-at-what-21-years-of-change-in-nonprofit-governance-looks-like). That makes the board meeting agenda the thing that decides how the limited time together gets spent. A clear one keeps directors focused on oversight and strategy. It lets them prepare. And it sets up the structure your minutes will follow.

Three practical benefits:

1. **Better decisions.** Directors who see the decision coming read the packet with that question in mind.
2. **Shorter meetings.** Time boxes and a consent agenda stop routine items from eating the meeting.
3. **Cleaner minutes.** Minutes that follow the agenda's numbering are easier to write and to check later.

## How do you create a board meeting agenda step by step?

Drafting a board meeting agenda costs the chair and CEO about an hour per meeting (industry estimate). Follow these 7 steps:

1. **Review last meeting's minutes** and open action items.
2. **List decisions the board must make**, and put them early in the agenda.
3. **Collect committee items** and any director requests.
4. **Sort items** into consent, discussion and decision.
5. **Add time boxes and owners** for each item.
6. **Attach supporting documents** to the board packet, with a one-line summary each.
7. **Send the agenda and packet** 5 to 7 days before the meeting.

Keep a standing agenda template. That way, the order of business stays put from one gathering to the next, and the packet work becomes routine: our [meeting preparation guide](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-preparation) sets out the rest of the prep.

## How does a good agenda make board minutes easier?

Number every item and state the decision needed. Then the secretary can write minutes straight into that structure, each agenda item becoming a minutes entry: a short summary, the motion, who moved and seconded, and the vote.

Search gets easier too. Say agenda item 7.2 approved the annual budget: minutes, packet and any follow-up all point back to 7.2, which is the whole idea behind [keeping meeting knowledge searchable](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-knowledge-management). ## What is a sample nonprofit board meeting agenda?

Nonprofit boards usually add a mission moment, fundraising and a financial review, as in this sample nonprofit board meeting agenda built around 90 minutes. California nonprofits should read it next to their bylaws, since [Corporations Code section 7211](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_7211) lets regular board meetings go ahead without notice when the bylaws fix the time and place.

```
1. Call to order and welcome (5 min)
2. Mission moment: program story (5 min)
3. Roll call and quorum (2 min)
4. Approval of agenda and prior minutes (3 min)
5. Consent agenda (2 min)
6. Executive Director report (15 min)
7. Treasurer's report: budget vs actual, cash position (15 min)
8. Fundraising and development update (10 min)
9. Strategic discussion: [topic] (20 min) | Decision: [ ]
10. Committee reports (8 min)
11. Executive session, if needed (5 min)
12. Adjournment
```

Why spend 5 minutes on a mission moment? It reminds directors why their decisions matter.

## What does a startup board meeting agenda look like?

Startup boards focus on metrics, strategy and approvals such as option grants. Here is a board meeting agenda example for a 2-hour venture-backed meeting:

```
1. Call to order, quorum, approval of prior minutes (5 min)
2. CEO update: highlights, lowlights, key metrics (20 min)
3. Financials: burn, runway, plan vs actual (15 min)
4. Deep dive: [go-to-market / product / hiring] (40 min)
5. Asks of the board: intros, advice (10 min)
6. Approvals: option grants, 409A, policies (10 min)
7. Executive session: board only (15 min)
8. Adjournment
```

Send the deck and metrics 3 to 5 days before. Why? The best startup board meetings spend most of the session on the deep dive, not reading slides aloud. ## What are board meeting agenda best practices?

Strong agendas give decisions and strategy at least half the meeting, not reports. These 6 practices get you there:

1. **Put decisions early.** Directors are sharpest in the first 30 minutes.
2. **Mark each item** as "for decision", "for discussion" or "for information".
3. **Move reports to the packet.** Presenters take questions instead of reading slides.
4. **Use a consent agenda** for routine approvals.
5. **Time-box every item** and name an owner.
6. **End with action items**, confirming who does what before the next meeting.

After each meeting, review the agenda. Has one item shown up 3 meetings in a row without a decision? Then it needs a different approach. Whatever the board commits to should leave the room as an [action item with an owner and a date](https://lynkk.ai/blog/action-items-examples-template).

## What is a consent agenda?

A consent agenda bundles routine, non-controversial items (approving minutes, committee reports, standard renewals) so the board approves them in one vote. Any director can ask to pull an item out for discussion.

Consent agendas clear space for strategy and decision-making. The catch? They only work if directors read the packet in advance.

## Who prepares the board meeting agenda?

Usually the board chair owns the agenda, preparing it with the CEO or executive director plus the secretary or governance staff, while committee chairs submit their items and directors can request topics through the chair.

Practical timeline for a monthly or quarterly board:

1. **2 weeks before:** chair and CEO agree on topics and decisions needed.
2. **10 days before:** committee chairs and staff submit reports.
3. **5 to 7 days before:** the agenda and board packet go to all directors.
4. **Meeting day:** the board approves the agenda at the start.

## How long should a board meeting be?

Most board meetings run 60 to 120 minutes (industry estimate), with nonprofit boards often meeting for 90 minutes monthly or quarterly and startup boards often taking 2 to 3 hours once a quarter. Regularly running over? Move reports into the packet and use a consent agenda.

## How do you turn the agenda into board minutes?

Board minutes follow the agenda order. Per item, record the decision, motion, mover and seconder, and vote. Reusing your agenda as a minutes template saves time. Nothing gets missed.

## FAQ

### What is the standard order of a board meeting agenda?

Call to order, roll call and quorum, approval of the agenda, approval of prior minutes, consent agenda, reports, old business, new business, executive session if needed, announcements and adjournment. Bylaws can change this order. Many boards also move key decisions earlier, so directors discuss them while they are fresh.

### Who sets the board meeting agenda?

The board chair sets the agenda, usually with the CEO or executive director and the board secretary. Directors can request items through the chair. Committee chairs submit their reports in advance, and the final version goes out with the packet so every director sees one identical plan.

### How far in advance should a board agenda be sent?

Send the agenda and packet 5 to 7 days ahead so directors can read the reports. State law sets only the floor for notice, not the packet: a California nonprofit calling a special meeting owes its directors 4 days by first-class mail, or 48 hours by phone, personal delivery or electronic transmission.

### Is an agenda required for a board meeting?

Notice is often required; an agenda often is not. California nonprofits are a clear case: Corporations Code section 7211 says a notice or waiver "need not specify the purpose" of any regular or special board meeting. Your bylaws may demand more than the statute, so read them before relying on this.

### What is the difference between old business and new business?

Old business covers items carried over; new business covers matters raised for the first time. One trap: moving to "table" an item does not park it until next month. Robert's Rules says Lay on the Table only sets a question aside temporarily, and Postpone to a Certain Time is the motion that moves it to a later meeting.

### What happens in executive session?

The board meets privately, often without staff, on CEO performance, legal matters or personnel. Minutes usually record only that the session happened and its general topic. Robert's Rules treats those proceedings as secret but not otherwise restricted, so a board can still take votes inside an executive session.
