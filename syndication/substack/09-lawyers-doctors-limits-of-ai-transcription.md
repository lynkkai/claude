# Lawyers, Doctors and the Limits of AI Transcription

*AI transcripts are good enough for most meetings. In law and medicine, "most" isn't the standard.*

Welcome back to **After the Call**. This week we're stepping into two professions where a transcript isn't just a convenience. It can be evidence, or part of a medical record.

*This issue is general information, not legal or medical compliance advice. Requirements differ by jurisdiction and by organization. Check with your compliance team or counsel before changing how you record.*

## Two kinds of transcript

It helps to separate two very different uses.

**Working transcripts** help professionals do their job: notes from a client intake call, a case strategy meeting, a clinical team huddle, a call with opposing counsel's office about scheduling. Accuracy matters, but a few errors are tolerable, because a human reads and uses the notes.

**Record transcripts** become part of an official record: court proceedings, depositions, sworn statements, a patient's medical record, documentation that supports billing. Here accuracy, formatting and chain of custody can be legal requirements.

AI is now genuinely useful for the first kind. For the second, the question is much harder.

## In law

For depositions and court proceedings, formal transcripts are generally produced by certified court reporters or official transcription processes, following specific formatting and certification rules. An AI-generated transcript isn't a substitute for those.

Where AI fits well in a law firm:

- **Client meetings and intake calls**, so the lawyer can focus on the client instead of typing;
- **Internal case strategy meetings**, where decisions and tasks need to be captured;
- **Reviewing long recordings** (interviews, hearings you have audio of) to find relevant passages quickly, with a human verifying anything you'll rely on.

The big caveats are privilege and confidentiality. Before using any AI tool on client conversations, a firm should know where the data goes, who can access it, whether the vendor trains models on it, and how long it's kept.

## In medicine

Clinical documentation is a heavy burden, and AI scribes and dictation tools have become popular for good reason. But healthcare adds strict requirements:

- In the US, tools that handle protected health information typically need to meet HIPAA requirements, which usually includes the vendor signing a **business associate agreement (BAA)**.
- Clinical notes become part of the medical record, so errors carry real risk.
- Medical vocabulary (drug names, dosages, anatomy) is where general-purpose transcription makes the most dangerous mistakes.

A general meeting notetaker isn't automatically suitable for clinical use, even if its transcripts are accurate. The questions to ask are about compliance and workflow, not just accuracy. Here's our guide to [medical transcription software and AI dictation](https://lynkk.ai/blog/medical-transcription-software), including what a HIPAA-compliant AI note taker needs and the categories of tools clinicians use.

Being straightforward about our own product: Lynkk is built for business meetings, not clinical documentation, and it doesn't offer a region setting for where data is processed today. If you work in healthcare, use tools designed and contracted for that purpose for anything involving patient information.

## The checklist for any regulated profession

Before using an AI transcription tool, ask the vendor, in writing:

1. Do you train models on our data?
2. Which third parties process our audio and transcripts?
3. Where is data processed and stored? Can we choose?
4. Will you sign the agreements our profession requires (for example a BAA)?
5. Who inside our organization can access a recording or transcript, and is access logged?
6. How do we delete data, and how quickly is it gone?

If the answers are vague, the tool isn't ready for your sensitive work, however good the transcripts are.

## This week's takeaway

AI transcription is excellent for working notes and still not a replacement for official records. Know which kind of transcript you're creating before you choose the tool.
