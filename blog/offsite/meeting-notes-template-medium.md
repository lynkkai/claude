Most meeting notes fail in the same place. Not during the meeting, after it.

The notes get written. They are accurate. Nobody can tell from reading them who owes what by when, so nothing moves, and three weeks later somebody reopens the same question in a new meeting. The fix is not writing more. It is writing the same four things every time, in the same order, so the reader's eye knows where to go.

Here is the template I use, and the reasoning behind each part. Copy it, change the headings to suit your team, and stop deciding what notes look like at 4pm on a Thursday.

## The template

```
Meeting:        [name]
Date:           [date]          Duration: [time]
Attendees:      [names]         Absent: [names]
Purpose:        [one sentence: why this meeting existed]

DECISIONS
1. [What was decided, in the past tense. No hedging.]
2. ...

ACTION ITEMS
| # | Action | Owner | Due |
|---|--------|-------|-----|
| 1 | [verb first] | [one name] | [date] |

DISCUSSION
- [Point raised] : [where it landed]
- ...

OPEN QUESTIONS
- [Question] : [who will answer it, by when]

Next meeting: [date, and the one thing it must resolve]
```

That is the whole thing. Four blocks, roughly a page. The structure does three jobs: it tells a reader who skipped the call what changed, it tells each person what they owe, and it stops the same topic from being rediscussed from scratch.

## Why decisions come first

Open any set of notes you have been sent this month. The decisions are probably buried in the middle of the discussion, phrased as "we talked about moving the launch", which is not a decision and cannot be acted on.

Put decisions at the top and write them in the past tense: "Launch moved to 14 March." The test is whether a sentence can be true or false. "Discussed the launch date" cannot be. "Launch moved to 14 March" can, which is exactly why it is useful: anyone who disagrees will say so immediately, while the meeting is still fresh, instead of six weeks later.

If a meeting produced no decisions, write "No decisions." It is uncomfortable to type, and it is the most valuable line in the document, because a run of those is data about your meeting schedule.

## One owner per action, and a real date

Two rules, and they carry most of the weight of the template.

**One name per action.** Not "Marketing", not "Sam and Priya", not "the team". A shared owner is an unowned action. If two people genuinely need to do two things, that is two rows.

**A date, not a horizon.** "Next sprint" and "ASAP" are not dates. The date is a commitment you can check, and checking is the point: next meeting opens with last meeting's table and each row marked done or not.

The verb comes first in the action text, which sounds fussy until you read fifty rows of it. "Send the revised quote to Acme" is a task. "Acme quote" is a label on something nobody will do. If you want a longer set of examples to steal from, there is a good collection of [action item examples](https://lynkk.ai/blog/action-items-examples-template) worth borrowing phrasing from.

## Discussion, compressed

This is the section that eats everyone's time, so constrain it. One line per point, in the form "point raised : where it landed". No transcript, no he-said-she-said, no reconstructing the argument.

The reason to keep anything at all is that decisions age badly without context. Six months on, "Launch moved to 14 March" reads as arbitrary. "Launch moved to 14 March : compliance review cannot finish before the 10th" reads as reasonable, and stops someone relitigating it. One clause of context is usually enough.

## Open questions: the section almost nobody writes

Most templates stop at action items. The result is a familiar failure: the thing blocking progress was not a task anybody could take, it was a question nobody could answer in the room, and because there was no place to write it down, it evaporated.

Give it a place. Each open question gets a name next to it, which is the person who will come back with an answer, not the person who will solve the problem. That distinction keeps the list honest and short.

## Three variants worth having

The same four blocks, tuned:

**One to ones.** Replace DECISIONS with "Since last time", and keep a running document rather than a new one each week. The value is the history, not the individual entry.

**Standups.** Compress to ACTION ITEMS and OPEN QUESTIONS only. If a standup is producing decisions, it is a different meeting wearing a standup's clothes.

**Formal or board meetings.** These are minutes, not notes, and the bar is different: attendance, quorum, who moved and who seconded, the exact text of resolutions. Several jurisdictions set rules about what minutes must contain and how fast they must be written, so if that is your situation, start from a [meeting minutes sample](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-minutes-sample) built for it rather than from this template. I wrote about the statutory side of that in the companion piece linked at the end.

## Writing it during the meeting, not after

The template only works if it gets filled in while people are still talking. Notes written from memory the next morning lose the thing that makes them valuable, which is precision about who said they would do what.

Two habits make that practical. Open the document before the meeting starts, with the four headings already in place and the purpose line written, so you are filling a form rather than facing a blank page. And in the last two minutes, read the action items out loud. Owners correct their own rows instantly; nobody corrects a document they received by email.

If you would rather not take notes by hand at all, this is the part software has genuinely got good at: an [AI meeting assistant](https://lynkk.ai/features/meeting-notes) can produce the decisions and the owner-and-date table for you, and you edit rather than compose. I work on one, so treat that as a disclosed bias rather than a neutral recommendation. The template matters either way, because a tool with no structure to aim at just gives you a shorter transcript.

## The actual test

A set of notes works if a person who missed the meeting can read it in under a minute and know what changed, what they owe, and when it is due. That is the whole standard. Everything above is just a layout that makes passing it the default rather than an achievement.
