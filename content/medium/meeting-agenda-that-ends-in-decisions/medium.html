How to Write a Meeting Agenda That Ends in Decisions

Turn every agenda item into a question the meeting has to answer, and start with what the last meeting left open.

AI disclosure: this story was drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed, fact-checked and edited by the author.

The invite goes out with a tidy agenda: "1. Q4 roadmap. 2. Hiring. 3. Any other business." The meeting fills the full hour. Everyone contributes. Then someone says, "Let's take this offline," and the group leaves without a single decision.

The agenda did what it was asked to do. It listed topics. The trouble is that a topic has no finish line. You can talk about "Q4 roadmap" for ten minutes or for ten weeks, and nothing on the page tells you when you're done.

Here is how to write a meeting agenda that does tell you: phrase each item as a question, say what kind of answer you need, and open with the work the last meeting left unfinished.

Having an agenda isn't the same as having a good one

Organizational psychologist Steven Rogelberg, who studies meetings, argues from his research that having an agenda does little on its own to make a meeting effective. What seems to matter is what's on it and how it's written.

That fits a common experience: plenty of unproductive meetings come with an agenda. It just doesn't say what the meeting is supposed to produce.

Write every agenda item as a question

A useful first change is to turn each topic into a question the group needs to answer. Roger Schwarz recommends exactly this in Harvard Business Review, and Rogelberg makes the same suggestion.

A few before-and-after examples:

  • "Q4 roadmap" becomes "Which two of the five proposed Q4 projects do we commit to?"
  • "Hiring" becomes "What should the new support hire own in their first month?"
  • "Customer churn" becomes "Do we add an exit survey, and if so, who builds it?"

A question does three jobs a topic can't:

  • It lets people prepare. "Which two projects?" tells everyone to read the proposals and arrive with a view.
  • It tells you who to invite. If a question needs the finance lead's judgment, the finance lead needs to be there. If it doesn't, maybe they don't.
  • It tells you when you're finished. Once the question has an answer, move on. If it can't be answered today, you know that too, and you can say what's missing.

If you can't turn an item into a question, that's useful information. It often means the item doesn't need a meeting.

Say what kind of answer you need

People participate differently depending on what's being asked of them. Schwarz suggests labeling each item by its purpose. A simple version uses three labels:

  • Inform: someone shares an update. Others listen and ask questions.
  • Input: someone wants opinions before deciding later.
  • Decide: the group, or a named person, will make a call in this meeting.

For every "Decide" item, write down who makes the decision. "Decide (Priya)" prevents the common ending where everyone assumed someone else had the final word.

The labels also expose a problem early. If an agenda has five "Decide" items and thirty minutes, it's a wish list. Cut it down or book more time.

Start with what the last meeting left open

Under Robert's Rules of Order, the standard order of business takes up unfinished business before new business. The reasoning holds for an ordinary team meeting too: if last week's commitments never come up again, people can start to treat them as optional.

So make the open action items from last time a standing agenda item, and give it the first few minutes. For each one, the owner answers in a sentence: done, blocked, or dropped.

  • Done: say so and move on.
  • Blocked: decide whether it needs an agenda item today or a follow-up afterward.
  • Dropped: say so out loud, so nobody keeps waiting on it.

This part is easy to skip, and worth keeping. It only works if the last meeting's action items were written down with owners, which is a good reason to take notes that way.

Decide what deserves meeting time at all

Schwarz's advice is to spend team meeting time mainly on issues that affect the whole team and need the whole team to resolve. Before an item goes on the agenda, three questions help:

  1. Does it need more than one person's judgment? If one person can decide, let them decide and share the result.
  2. Does everyone invited need to be part of it? If only two people do, it belongs in a smaller conversation.
  3. Could it be settled in writing? Most "Inform" items can be sent as a message. Keep one on the agenda only if you expect real questions.

Asking for agenda items from the team helps too. Schwarz suggests that people say why their item needs the group. That one sentence can weed out items before they cost anyone an hour. A short email is enough: "Agenda for Thursday: reply by Wednesday noon with your item, the question you want answered, and why it needs the group."

Give each item a realistic time

Put a time next to every item. Schwarz notes that leaders typically underestimate how long a topic needs once you count introducing it, answering questions, working through disagreement and agreeing on next steps.

Two habits help:

  • Put the hardest decision near the start, while people are fresh and before smaller items expand to fill the time. This is advice rather than a rule, but the alternative is often a rushed decision in the last five minutes.
  • Decide in advance what happens if time runs out. "If we haven't decided by 2:45, Priya makes the call by Friday" is better than an open-ended "let's revisit."

A team meeting agenda example you can reuse

Here is the whole approach as a simple meeting agenda outline, with last meeting's action items at the top. It fits in a calendar invite.

Team sync: Thu Oct 9, 30 min
Goal: agree on Q4 launch scope

OPEN FROM LAST TIME (5 min)
- Ana: pricing page copy (done / blocked / dropped?)
- Sam: customer email draft (done / blocked / dropped?)

1. Which two of the five Q4 projects do we commit to?
   DECIDE (Priya) / 15 min
   Read before: one-page proposal (link)

2. What should the new support hire own in month one?
   INPUT (Sam decides by Mon) / 7 min

3. Billing change timeline
   INFORM / 3 min (or send as a message)

(This is an illustrative example, not a real meeting.)

Send it at least a day ahead with any reading attached, so the "Read before" line means something.

The agenda tells you when you're done

When every item is a question, the meeting is done once each one is answered, or parked with an owner and a date. Those answers become the decisions in your notes, and the parked items become next week's "Open from last time."
