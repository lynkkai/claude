# Action Items Die in the Chat. Here Are 5 Rules That Keep Them Alive.

*This story was written with AI assistance and edited by the Lynkk team.*

There's a moment near the end of most meetings when someone says, "Great, so we'll follow up on that." Everyone nods. The call ends. And the follow-up quietly dies in a chat thread nobody scrolls back to.

It isn't laziness. Most action items fail for structural reasons, which is good news, because structural problems have structural fixes.

Here are five rules. None of them needs new software. All of them work better with it.

## Rule 1: An action item has exactly one owner

"Marketing will look into it" is not an action item. Neither is "we should." When a task belongs to a team, it belongs to nobody.

Write a name. One name. If two people truly share the work, one of them still owns making sure it happens.

This one change fixes more follow-through problems than any tool. If you only adopt one rule, adopt this one.

## Rule 2: It starts with a verb and ends with a date

Compare:

- "Pricing page"
- "Dana to send the revised pricing page draft to Sam by Thursday"

The first is a topic. The second is a commitment. A useful action item answers three questions: **who** does **what** by **when**.

If nobody can name a date, ask why. Usually the honest answer is "we haven't decided whether to do it," which means it's an open question, not an action item. Write it down as one.

There's a longer list of [action item examples and a copyable template](https://lynkk.ai/blog/action-items-examples-template) on the Lynkk blog, if you want to see good and bad versions side by side.

## Rule 3: Capture them in the meeting, not after

Memory decays fast. If you rebuild the list from memory an hour later, you'll get the big items and lose the small ones, and the small ones are usually what blocks the big ones.

Two ways to do this:

1. **Assign a scribe** whose only job is to write down decisions and action items as they're said, and read them back in the last two minutes.
2. **Record the call** and let a note taker pull out the action items, then spend the last two minutes confirming them out loud.

Either way, the read-back is the important part. "Here's what I have, is anything missing or wrong?" catches most mistakes while everyone is still in the room.

## Rule 4: Send them where the work already lives

This is where most teams lose. The action items are written down, and then they live in a Google Doc, a Notion page or a chat message, which is to say nowhere anyone looks on a Tuesday.

Action items should land in the place where that kind of work is tracked:

- engineering tasks in Jira or your issue tracker;
- customer commitments in the CRM or the account plan;
- personal follow-ups in the owner's own task list.

For product and engineering teams, sending action items straight into Jira closes the loop. We wrote up [how teams turn meeting minutes into Jira issues](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-minutes-to-jira), including what to put in the issue so it still makes sense to someone who wasn't on the call.

## Rule 5: Close the loop in writing within 24 hours

The follow-up message is where an agreement becomes official. Without it, everyone leaves with a slightly different memory of what was decided.

Keep it short:

> **Decided:** We'll launch the pilot with 15 users on the 28th.
> **Action items:**
> Dana: revised pricing draft to Sam, Thursday.
> Dev: QA sign-off on the pilot build, Friday.
> **Open:** Who owns the customer training session?

That's it. Five lines. Send it to everyone who attended, plus anyone who owns an item but wasn't there. If you send these to clients or prospects, the tone matters more; there are [12 follow-up email examples](https://lynkk.ai/blog/follow-up-email-after-meeting) for different situations if you need a starting point.

## Why the chat thread is the worst place for action items

It's tempting to post the follow-up in the team chat and call it done. The trouble is that chat is designed to move. Today's action items get buried by tomorrow's jokes, and nobody can check off a chat message.

Use chat to *announce* the follow-up. Use your task tracker to *hold* it.

## The two-week test

Here's how to tell whether your action items are working. Take any meeting from two weeks ago and ask:

1. Can I find the list of action items in under a minute?
2. Does every item have one owner and a date?
3. How many are done?

If you can't answer the first question, start with Rule 4. If you can't answer the second, start with Rules 1 and 2. If the third answer is "almost none," the meeting probably agreed to things nobody actually intended to do, and that's a conversation worth having in the next meeting.
