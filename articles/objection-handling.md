# Objection Handling: Frameworks, Scripts and Live Examples

By [Samarth Wagh](https://lynkk.ai/blog/authors/samarth-wagh) · 4 November 2026 · Research checked against the linked sources in November 2026

> **Quick answer:** Objection handling is how a sales rep responds to a buyer's concern so the deal keeps moving. Use the four LAER steps: listen, acknowledge, explore with a question, then respond. In Gong's study of **67,149 demo calls**, top reps paused and asked a question, while average reps talked for about **21 seconds** straight.

Handling objections well is the skill of turning "no, because" into "yes, if". Most objections aren't refusals at all. They're the buyer telling you what's missing. Find out what that is, and you usually win the deal.

## What is objection handling in sales?

In sales, handling an objection means hearing a buyer's concern, finding the real reason behind it, and answering it so the call moves to a next step. A good answer lowers the buyer's risk. A bad one starts an argument.

An objection isn't a rejection. "We already have a tool for this" leaves the door open, while "we've signed a three-year contract with someone else" mostly shuts it. Telling the two apart, fast, is half the job.

## Why is objection handling important in sales?

Handling objections decides most late-stage deals, because almost every buyer raises at least one concern before they sign. [Gong Labs analysed 67,149 demo calls](https://www.gong.io/blog/handling-sales-objections) and found a clear split: average reps answered an objection with a 21.45-second monologue, while top reps slowed down and asked questions.

That gap is the whole lesson. Objections rarely kill deals on their own. The rush to answer does, because the rep ends up solving a problem the buyer never actually had.

## What are the most common sales objections?

Most sales objections fall into 4 groups: price, need, timing and trust. [HubSpot's list of 44 common objections](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/handling-common-sales-objections) sorts them much the same way, and knowing the group tells you which question to ask first.

| Objection group | What the buyer says | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Price | "It's too expensive." "We don't have budget." | The value isn't clear yet, or the wrong person holds the budget |
| Need | "We're fine with what we have." "Not interested." | They don't see the problem, or don't think it's urgent |
| Timing | "Call me next quarter." "I need to think about it." | Something else is competing for attention, or a risk is unspoken |
| Trust and authority | "I need to run it past my boss." "Send me some info." | They can't sign alone, or don't yet trust the claim |

## What is the best objection handling framework?

The most widely used objection handling framework is LAER, which stands for listen, acknowledge, explore and respond. Carew International developed it and still sells training on it, per [Think Insights' summary of the LAER model](https://thinkinsights.net/commercial-excellence/laer-model). It works for a simple reason: step three forces a question before you're allowed to answer.

| Framework | Steps | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| LAER | Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond | Any objection, and the default for B2B |
| LAARC | Listen, Acknowledge, Assess, Respond, Confirm | Complex deals where you need a clear yes before moving on |
| Feel, felt, found | "I understand how you feel. Others felt the same. They found..." | Emotional or risk objections, with a real customer story |
| Boomerang | Turn the objection into the reason to buy | Objections that are really a strength in disguise |

### What are the 5 steps of objection handling?

The 5 steps are: pause, clarify, isolate, answer and confirm. The [National Association of Sales Professionals](https://www.nasp.com/blog/the-5-step-method-of-handling-objections) teaches a similar five-step method, and it maps cleanly onto LAER with a check at the end.

1. **Pause:** let the buyer finish, then wait a beat before you speak.
2. **Clarify:** ask what they mean. "Too expensive compared to what?"
3. **Isolate:** check it's the only blocker. "If price were sorted, is there anything else?"
4. **Answer:** respond to the real concern, with proof, in two or three sentences.
5. **Confirm:** ask if that answered it, then agree the next step.

## What should you say to the most common objections?

Say less than you think you need to. Lead with a question.

Each objection handling script below opens with a question, then answers in one or two lines. Change the wording to fit your product and your own voice. Buyers can hear a script being read.

### How do you handle "it's too expensive"?

Handle a price objection by asking what they're comparing the price to, then showing the cost of the problem staying unsolved. "Too expensive" almost always means "I can't see the return yet," which is why [HubSpot's price objection guide](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/price-objection-responses) also starts with value, not discounts.

> **Try:** "Fair question. Compared to what, another tool, or doing nothing? ... If it saves each rep two hours a week, what's that worth to your team over a quarter?"

### How do you handle "I need to think about it"?

Treat "I need to think about it" as a sign that something is unspoken, and ask what specifically they want to think through. Name the concern now, and you won't lose the deal to silence later.

> **Try:** "Of course. So I can send the right thing, what's the part you want to think through, the price, the rollout, or whether it fits the team?"

### How do you handle "I'm not interested"?

Respond to "I'm not interested" with a short, specific reason to keep talking, then ask permission for one question. On a cold call, each sentence has to earn the next one.

> **Try:** "Totally fair, you weren't expecting me. Most sales leaders I talk to are losing deal notes between calls. Is that a problem for your team, or not really?"

### How do you handle "we don't have budget"?

Answer a no-budget objection by finding out whether the budget is missing, already spent, or held by someone else. Each needs a different next step. Only the first is a real no.

> **Try:** "Understood. Is that no budget this year, or no budget line for this yet? Who usually decides when something new comes up mid-year?"

### How do you handle "I don't have time"?

Handle a no-time objection by shrinking the ask and booking a specific slot. "Let's talk later" with no date attached is how deals quietly die.

> **Try:** "I get it. Would 15 minutes on Thursday at 10 work, just to see if this is worth a proper look? If not, I'll leave you alone."

## What does objection handling look like on a live call?

Here are 2 short, realistic objection handling examples, written the way they sound on a call. Both follow LAER, and both end with a confirmed next step rather than a clever line.

**Example 1: a price objection on a renewal call**

> **Buyer:** "Honestly, your renewal is 20% higher than last year. That's hard to justify."
>
> **Rep:** "That's a real jump, and I'd push back too. Can I ask what you're measuring it against, last year's price or what the team gets out of it?"
>
> **Buyer:** "Last year's price, mostly. Finance just sees the number."
>
> **Rep:** "Makes sense. Last quarter your team logged 340 calls through us. If I put that next to the cost per call for finance, would that help you make the case?"
>
> **Buyer:** "Yes, that would actually help."

**Example 2: a timing objection on a discovery call**

> **Buyer:** "This looks good, but we're not looking at anything until Q2."
>
> **Rep:** "Thanks for telling me straight. What's happening between now and Q2, a budget cycle or another project?"
>
> **Buyer:** "We're migrating our CRM. Nobody has bandwidth."
>
> **Rep:** "That's useful. Would it be worth a 20-minute call in March to plan around the migration, so you're not starting from zero in April?"

The numbers in these examples are illustrative. The shape is what carries over: acknowledge, ask, then answer the real concern with something the buyer can actually use.

## How do you handle objections on a cold call?

On a cold call, handle objections with one acknowledgement and one question, and never more than about 20 seconds of talking. They didn't ask for the call. Earning another minute is hard.

The most common cold call objections are "not interested", "send me an email" and "we already use someone". For each one, agree first, then ask a single question that tests whether the problem even exists.

Clear no? Thank them and hang up. You'll get a warmer hearing next time.

## How do you practice objection handling?

The fastest way to practice is to replay your own recorded calls, find every objection, and rewrite your answer using LAER. Role-play helps. But real objections from real buyers beat anything a manager can invent.

Try this weekly drill with your team:

1. **Collect:** pull 5 objections from last week's recorded calls.
2. **Sort:** tag each one as price, need, timing or trust.
3. **Rewrite:** draft a better first question for each one.
4. **Role-play:** run each objection twice, swapping buyer and rep.
5. **Track:** check next week's calls to see if the new lines landed.

### What mistakes do reps make when handling objections?

Reps mostly lose deals by answering too fast and for too long. Five mistakes turn up in call review after call review:

- **Talking over the pause:** jumping in before the buyer has finished.
- **Asking "why":** it sounds like a challenge. Ask "what" or "how" instead.
- **Answering the wrong objection:** solving price when the real blocker is authority.
- **Discounting first:** cutting price before you know what the objection means.
- **Skipping the confirm:** moving on without checking the answer landed.

## FAQ

### Is objection handling a soft skill?

Yes. It's a soft skill built on listening, empathy and asking good questions, but it can be trained like a hard one. Reps improve fastest when they review real call recordings, practise specific objections with a colleague, and measure whether their new responses actually move deals to a next step.

### What is the feel, felt, found method?

Feel, felt, found is one of the oldest objection handling techniques, and it has three parts. You say you understand how the buyer feels, that other customers felt the same, and what those customers found after buying. It only works with a true customer story, so keep one or two real examples ready for each common objection.

### Are objections buying signals?

Often, yes. A buyer who raises an objection is still engaged enough to explain what's stopping them, which is better than polite silence. Questions about price, rollout or contracts usually mean they're picturing the purchase. The risk is treating every objection as a buying signal and missing a real no.

### What is an objection handling matrix?

An objection matrix is a table that lists common objections, what each one usually means, and a tested response for each. Teams keep it in a shared doc or sales playbook. The best matrices are built from objections heard on real recorded calls, and updated whenever a better response wins a deal.

### How do you handle a price objection over email?

Keep it short and ask one question. Thank them for being direct, ask what they're comparing the price to, and offer a 15-minute call to walk through the return. Avoid sending a discount by email, because it anchors the price lower before you know what the objection really means.

### How do you handle objections in customer service?

In customer service, use the same LAER steps but slow down even more. Let the customer finish, acknowledge the frustration without blaming anyone, ask a question to find the real problem, then offer a specific fix with a time frame. The goal is a solved problem, not a won argument.

## Conclusion

Handling objections is less about the perfect line and more about the pause and the question that come before it. Learn LAER. Keep a few honest scripts. Then review your real calls, every week, without skipping.
