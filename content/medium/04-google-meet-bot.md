# Google Meet Bot Etiquette: How to Add, Admit or Block a Note-Taking Bot

## What a meeting bot is, how it joins, and the unwritten rules that keep everyone comfortable

A Google Meet bot is a note-taking participant that an AI service sends into your call to record it and write notes. It joins like a guest: it opens the meeting link, asks to join, and waits for someone to admit it. Once inside, it appears in the participant list under its own name.

Bots are now common enough that most people have met one, and opinions are mixed. Some teams love walking out of a call with notes already written. Others find an unexplained bot in the room unsettling. This guide covers how a meeting bot joins, how to send one, when hosts should admit or remove one, and the etiquette that makes bots useful instead of awkward.

## How a Google Meet bot joins a call

The sequence is the same for almost every note-taking bot:

1. **You give the bot a link.** Either you paste it into the service, or the service reads it from your calendar.
2. **The bot opens the link and asks to join.** In Google Meet this looks like any guest knocking.
3. **Someone in the meeting admits it.** Usually the host, but often any participant can, depending on settings.
4. **The bot records until the meeting ends** or until someone removes it.

The bot needs only the meeting link. It does not need your Google password, and it cannot see your mailbox or files.

## Why a Google Meet bot joiner gets stuck

People search for a "google meet bot joiner" because the bot often sits outside the call. The usual causes:

- **Nobody is in the meeting yet.** The bot knocks, but there is no one to let it in.
- **Meeting access is restricted.** Google Meet's host controls (Quick access and Host management in many Workspace accounts) decide who can join without asking. Locked-down meetings may never show the request.
- **Company policy blocks outside guests.** Some Workspace admins only allow people from their own organization.
- **The link changed.** An edited invite can leave the bot knocking on an old meeting.

If a bot cannot get in, that is often the meeting working as designed. The fix is to ask the host, not to look for a workaround.

## Does it work the same way in Zoom and Teams?

Mostly, yes. The same bot usually joins all three platforms from a link, and the same etiquette applies. The difference is in the door:

- **Zoom** uses a waiting room. If it is on, the host or a co-host clicks Admit for the bot, just like for a person.
- **Microsoft Teams** uses a lobby. Company policies decide who skips it, and some organizations block anonymous guests entirely, which keeps every outside bot out.
- **Google Meet** shows a join request. Whoever can admit guests in that meeting can let the bot in.

So if your bot works in one platform but not another, the cause is almost always the meeting's access settings, not the bot.

## The etiquette of bringing a bot to someone else's meeting

Here is where most of the friction comes from. Sending your bot to a meeting you do not run is like bringing a guest to a dinner: ask first.

- **Tell the host before the call.** A one-line message works: "Mind if my note-taker joins? I'll share the notes."
- **Name it clearly.** If you can set the bot's display name, use something like "Sam's notes (recording)".
- **Say it out loud at the start.** Even if everyone can see the bot, a spoken heads-up removes any doubt.
- **Offer the notes.** A bot that only benefits you feels like surveillance. One that shares a summary with everyone feels like help.
- **Do not send a bot to a meeting you skip** unless the host agrees. A bot in place of a person changes the tone of a small meeting.

## When hosts should admit or remove a bot

If you run the meeting, you decide. A simple policy saves awkward moments:

- **Admit it** when the attendee asked in advance, the meeting is routine, and notes will be shared.
- **Ask first** when an unexpected bot knocks. "Whose note-taker is this?" in the chat is a fair question.
- **Remove it** for sensitive topics such as HR issues, legal advice, negotiations or anything under NDA, unless everyone agrees.

Many university and company IT teams now publish guidance on bots that join meetings automatically from an attendee's calendar. Their advice is consistent: use the waiting room, check unknown participants, and remove anything you did not expect.

## How to send a meeting bot to a meeting

If you want a bot in your own meetings, the setup takes a couple of minutes:

1. **Choose a note taker** that supports the platforms you use. Most cover Google Meet, Zoom and Microsoft Teams.
2. **Send it one meeting first** by pasting the link. Check what the notes look like before you automate anything.
3. **Turn on calendar auto-join** once you trust it, so it joins every meeting with a link.
4. **Exclude sensitive meetings** from auto-join, such as one-on-ones or interviews, or set the bot to ask first.

For example, the [Lynkk](https://lynkk.ai) meeting bot joins Google Meet, Zoom and Teams links, either pasted or from calendar auto-join. It is free and records audio, shows a live transcript during the call, and turns each recording into a Meeting Brief with decisions and action items. When a meeting does not allow bots, Lynkk's Chrome extension records your own browser tab instead, with no bot in the call. (Full disclosure: I work on Lynkk.)

## When to skip the bot entirely

A bot is not always the right tool. Consider recording from your own side, or not recording at all, when:

- **The meeting is small and personal,** like a one-on-one where a visible bot changes the conversation.
- **The host has said no.** Respect it, and take notes by hand.
- **The other side is a customer or candidate** who may feel uncomfortable. Ask, and accept a no.

If you do record from your own computer, the consent duty is all yours, because nobody sees a bot in the list.

## Is it legal to use a meeting bot?

A visible bot helps with consent, but it does not replace it. In the US, federal law allows recording with the consent of at least one participant ([18 U.S.C. § 2511](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title18-section2511)), while several states require everyone to agree. In the EU, a recording of identifiable people is personal data under the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj).

The safe habit everywhere is the same: announce the recording at the start, and stop if someone objects. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Google Meet bot etiquette checklist

- Ask the host before sending your bot to their meeting.
- Give the bot a clear name that says it is recording.
- Announce it out loud at the start.
- Share the notes with everyone who attended.
- Hosts: admit expected bots, question unexpected ones, remove them for sensitive topics.
- Exclude one-on-ones and interviews from auto-join.
- If the host says no, take notes another way.

Bots are useful when everyone knows they are there and gets something from them.
