# Personal Knowledge Management: Systems, Methods and How to Start

By [Samarth Wagh](https://lynkk.ai/blog/authors/samarth-wagh) · 4 November 2026 · Methods checked against their primary sources in November 2026

> **Quick answer:** Personal knowledge management (PKM) is how you capture, organise and reuse what you learn. **3** methods cover almost all of it: PARA sorts notes by how actionable they are, Zettelkasten links small notes written in your own words, and CODE names the workflow. Luhmann's Zettelkasten sat behind **50** books and over **600** articles.

PKM is the practice of collecting what you learn, organising it so you can find it again, and turning it into something you produce. It's the system behind the app. Which is why people who switch tools every six months still feel just as disorganised as they did before.

## What is personal knowledge management (PKM)?

PKM is a deliberate process for the information you take in: what you capture, where it goes, and what you do with it. PKM is the usual abbreviation, and the "personal" part matters, because your system only has to work for you.

It has 4 moving parts, whatever method you follow:

1. **Capture:** getting ideas out of your head and into one trusted place.
2. **Organise:** putting them where you'll find them again.
3. **Distill:** rewriting what matters in your own words.
4. **Express:** using it in work you actually produce.

## Why does PKM matter?

Knowledge you can't retrieve is the same as knowledge you never had. The point isn't a tidy archive. It's shortening the gap between learning something and using it.

The best-known evidence is Niklas Luhmann, the sociologist whose paper slip-box sat behind **50 books and over 600 articles**, [according to Zettelkasten.de](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/). He also left more than 150 unfinished manuscripts, so the system clearly wasn't magic. What it did mean is that he never started from a blank page.

## What are the main personal knowledge management methods, and how do they differ?

The 3 main PKM methods are PARA, Zettelkasten and CODE. Each answers a different question, so most people end up combining them rather than picking one.

| Method | Answers | Core idea | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| PARA | Where does this note go? | Sort by how actionable it is | People with projects and deadlines |
| Zettelkasten | How do ideas connect? | Small linked notes in your own words | Writers and researchers |
| CODE | What's the workflow? | Capture, organise, distill, express | Anyone starting out |

### What is the PARA method?

PARA sorts everything into 4 folders by how actionable it is, not by subject. [Tiago Forte](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/) defines Projects as "short-term efforts... that you take on with a certain goal in mind" and Areas as "important parts of your work and life that require ongoing attention". Resources covers "a range of topics you're interested in and learning about", and Archives holds anything "no longer active".

Why does it work? Subject folders grow forever, while project folders empty themselves. A project ends, its notes move to Archives, and your working space stays small.

### What is the Zettelkasten method?

Zettelkasten means slip box, and the method is to write small notes in your own words and link them to each other, so connections build over time. Zettelkasten.de sets out 3 guiding principles: one idea per note, write in your own language, and connect each new note to related ones while saying why the connection matters.

That last part is the method. As Zettelkasten.de puts it, "a Zettelkasten makes connecting and not collecting a priority", which is the opposite of saving 400 articles you never open.

### What is the CODE method?

CODE describes the workflow rather than the filing: capture, organise, distill, express. It comes from Tiago Forte's Building a Second Brain, and it pairs with PARA, which handles the organise step.

Capture what resonates, organise it by actionability, distill it to the part you'd want to read later, and express it in work you produce. Distill is the step people skip. It's also the one that turns a pile of highlights into something usable.

## What is the difference between personal knowledge management and a second brain?

In the personal knowledge management vs second brain debate, PKM is the practice and a second brain is one popular name for the system you build. PKM is the older, broader term. "Second brain" comes from Tiago Forte's book, and usually implies the CODE and PARA approach specifically.

Day to day, they overlap almost completely. If you're comparing tools rather than methods, our guide to the [best second brain apps](https://lynkk.ai/blog/best-second-brain-apps) compares Obsidian, Notion, Logseq and the rest on price and fit.

## What does a PKM system look like in practice?

A working personal knowledge management system has one inbox, a small set of folders, a weekly review and a place where output happens. Here's a personal knowledge management example you can copy in an afternoon.

| Part | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inbox | One place everything lands | A single note called Inbox, or your app's daily note |
| Folders | PARA's four, nothing more | Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives |
| Notes | Short, in your own words | One idea per note, with a link to what it relates to |
| Review | A weekly pass over the inbox | 20 minutes on Friday: file, distill or delete |
| Output | Where you use it | Drafts, briefs, decisions, code |

## How do you start a PKM system?

Start with one inbox and a weekly review, and nothing else. Add structure before the capture habit, and the system dies in week three.

1. **Week 1:** capture everything into one note. Don't organise it.
2. **Week 2:** keep capturing, and add a 20-minute Friday review.
3. **Week 3:** create PARA's four folders, and file from the inbox.
4. **Week 4:** distill 3 notes into your own words and use one in real work.

Survived a month? Then ask whether the app is holding you back. Most people find it never was the problem.

## What are the most common PKM mistakes?

The most common mistake is collecting instead of connecting, which produces an archive you never reopen. These 5 come up again and again:

- **Hoarding:** saving articles as a substitute for reading them.
- **Over-structuring:** building a taxonomy before you have 50 notes.
- **Tool-hopping:** migrating apps instead of writing notes.
- **Copy-pasting:** quoting sources rather than writing in your own words.
- **No review:** capture with no weekly pass, so the inbox becomes a graveyard.

## How does AI change a PKM workflow?

AI mostly changes retrieval. Instead of remembering where you filed something, you ask a question across everything you've saved and get an answer with its source attached.

It doesn't replace the distill step. A summary written by a model isn't the same as a note you wrote yourself, because the understanding comes from the writing. Use AI to find and connect, and keep writing the notes that matter in your own words.

## FAQ

### What is a PKM system in simple terms?

A PKM system is your personal setup for keeping what you learn and finding it again. At minimum it's one place to capture notes, a simple way to sort them, and a regular review. It can be an app with backlinks or a folder of text files. The habit of reviewing matters far more than the software.

### Which PKM method is best for beginners?

Start with PARA and the CODE workflow, because both are simple and need no special software. Create four folders, capture into one inbox, and review weekly. Zettelkasten rewards patience and dense linking, so it suits writers and researchers more than someone starting their first system this month.

### Is Zettelkasten better than PARA?

They solve different problems, so neither is better. PARA decides where a note lives based on how actionable it is, which suits project work. Zettelkasten decides how notes connect, which suits long-term thinking and writing. Many people use PARA for folders and Zettelkasten habits for the notes themselves.

### How many notes should a PKM system have?

There's no target, and chasing a number is a warning sign. Luhmann's slip box held tens of thousands of notes over decades, but it supported his writing rather than existing for its own sake. A system with 200 notes you actually reuse beats one with 5,000 you never reopen.

### Do you need an app for your notes?

No. A folder of plain text files, or a single notebook, works if you review it. Apps help as the archive grows, mainly through search, backlinks and sync. Pick one that exports cleanly, so you can leave without losing anything, and spend your effort on the habit rather than the setup.

### How does organisational knowledge management differ from PKM?

Organisational knowledge management is how a company captures and shares what its people know, usually through wikis, documentation and process. PKM is the individual version, with no committee to satisfy. The two meet when your personal notes become the source for something your whole team relies on.

## Conclusion

A knowledge management workflow is less about the app and more about four habits: capture in one place, organise by what's actionable, distill in your own words, and review weekly. Then add meetings, because that's where most of your knowledge gets created, and lost.
