# Your Meeting Notes Are Not Your Minutes (and Neither Is Your Memory)

*This story was written with AI assistance and edited by the Lynkk team.*

Every organization keeps some record of its meetings. Very few can say what that record is for.

That sounds pedantic until you watch what happens. A board asks to see the minutes and gets someone's bullet points. A new hire asks why a decision was made and gets a link to a 50-minute recording. A manager writes beautiful notes that nobody but the manager ever reads.

The fix starts with a distinction most people skip. There are three different records a meeting can leave behind, and each one has a different job.

## Record 1: Notes are for the people in the room

Meeting notes are working material. They are informal, often personal, and written to help the people who were there remember and act.

Good notes are short. They capture:

- what was decided,
- who is doing what, and by when,
- what is still open.

Notes don't need to be complete. They need to be useful by tomorrow morning. If you have to read your notes twice to work out what you're supposed to do, they failed.

## Record 2: Minutes are for the people who weren't

Minutes are a formal record. Boards, committees, nonprofits, school councils and regulated businesses keep them because someone outside the meeting (an auditor, a member, a court, a future board) may need to rely on them.

That changes what goes in. Minutes record who attended, whether there was a quorum, the motions made, who moved and seconded them, and how the vote went. They are approved at the next meeting. They usually *don't* record every argument, and in many organizations they shouldn't.

The most common mistake is treating minutes like long notes. They aren't. They are closer to a legal summary of actions taken. If you've never written them, it helps to start from a real [meeting minutes sample](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-minutes-sample) rather than a blank page.

For the full side-by-side, including why they're called minutes at all, the Lynkk team wrote a guide to [meeting notes vs minutes](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-notes-vs-minutes) and how to turn one into the other.

## Record 3: Memory is for the team six months from now

Here's the record almost nobody keeps on purpose.

Six months after a meeting, nobody needs the notes or the minutes. They need an answer:

- "Why did we drop the annual plan?"
- "What did the customer say about their security review?"
- "Did we ever agree on a launch date with marketing?"

Notes are too scattered to answer that. Minutes are too formal. Recordings are too long. What you need is the ability to search across many meetings and get an answer with a pointer to where it was said.

This is what people mean when they talk about "knowledge management" for meetings. It's less about software than about one property: **can someone who wasn't there find the reason for a decision without asking the person who was?** If not, your team's memory sits in people's heads, and it leaves when they do.

We've written about the [tools teams use to turn meetings into searchable memory](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-knowledge-management), from wikis to AI search with cited answers.

## Why one record can't do all three jobs

Teams usually pick one of these and stretch it.

**Notes stretched into minutes** miss the formal parts (attendance, motions, votes) and include things that shouldn't be in a permanent record.

**Minutes stretched into notes** are too slow. By the time they're approved, the action items are a week old.

**Recordings stretched into memory** technically contain everything and practically contain nothing, because no one will scrub through them.

Each record has a different reader, a different deadline and a different level of formality:

| Record | Reader | Deadline | Formality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notes | People in the room | Within hours | Low |
| Minutes | People who weren't there, plus auditors | Next meeting | High |
| Memory | The team, months later | Whenever the question comes up | Searchable, cited |

## A simple setup that covers all three

You don't need a new system. You need each record to have an owner and a default.

1. **Notes: automatic by default.** Capture the call (by hand or with a note taker) and send a short summary with decisions and owners the same day.
2. **Minutes: only where they're required.** For a board or committee, one named person drafts minutes from the notes using a fixed template, and the group approves them at the next meeting.
3. **Memory: one searchable place.** Every set of notes goes into the same archive. Not ten personal folders. If your tool can answer questions across meetings and show the source, use it. If not, a shared, consistently named folder is a fine start.

The test is the six-month question. Pick a decision your team made last quarter and try to find out why it was made, without asking anyone. How long did it take?

If the answer is "I gave up," you don't have a notes problem. You have a memory problem.
