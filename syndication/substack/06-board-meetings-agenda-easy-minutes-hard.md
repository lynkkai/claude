# Board Meetings: The Agenda Is Easy. The Minutes Are Hard.

*Founders spend days on the board deck and minutes on the minutes. That's backwards.*

Welcome back to **After the Call**. This week's issue is for founders, executive directors and anyone who has ever been told "you'll need to take the minutes" five minutes before a board meeting.

*A quick note: this is general information, not legal advice. Requirements for board minutes depend on your jurisdiction, your entity type and your bylaws. If in doubt, ask your company lawyer or secretary.*

## The agenda gets all the attention

Most of the energy before a board meeting goes into the deck: metrics, narrative, the ask. The agenda is usually a copy of last quarter's with dates changed. That's fine. Board agendas are fairly standard: call to order, approval of the previous minutes, reports, old business, new business, any votes, adjournment.

If you need a starting point, we published a [board meeting agenda template](https://lynkk.ai/blog/board-meeting-agenda) for US boards, with startup and nonprofit samples and how consent agendas work.

The minutes get almost none of that attention. Someone types notes during the meeting, cleans them up a week later, and they're approved at the next meeting without much reading.

## Why the minutes matter more than the deck

The deck is forgotten within a quarter. The minutes are permanent.

Board minutes are the official record of what the board decided: approvals of option grants, financings, budgets, officer appointments, major contracts. They're what lawyers, auditors and future investors look at during due diligence. When a question comes up years later ("did the board actually approve that grant?"), the minutes are the answer.

Badly kept minutes create real problems: missing approvals that have to be ratified later, unclear records of who voted how, and in some cases, discussions recorded in more detail than anyone wanted.

## Notes are not minutes

The most common mistake is treating minutes as detailed notes. They're not. Minutes record **what the board did**, not everything the board said.

Typically, minutes include:

- the date, time and place, and whether it was in person or virtual;
- who attended (directors, observers, guests) and whether a quorum was present;
- approval of the previous meeting's minutes;
- each motion, who made and seconded it, and the outcome of the vote;
- any director who abstained or recused themselves;
- the time of adjournment.

They usually *don't* include a transcript of the debate. Many lawyers advise keeping discussion summaries short and factual. We wrote a longer explainer on [meeting notes vs minutes](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-notes-vs-minutes) and how to turn one into the other.

## A practical routine

Here's a routine that works for most early-stage boards:

1. **Assign a secretary** before the meeting. Not the person presenting. Often a company lawyer, the COO or a designated staff member.
2. **Prepare a minutes skeleton** from the agenda: every item and every expected vote already listed, with blanks for outcomes.
3. **Capture the meeting** (with the board's agreement), whether by careful notes or a recording, so the secretary can focus on motions and votes rather than typing everything.
4. **Draft the minutes within 48 hours**, while memories are fresh. Use the skeleton, fill in outcomes, keep discussion summaries brief.
5. **Have counsel review** anything involving equity, financing or conflicts of interest.
6. **Circulate the draft** with the next meeting's materials and approve it as the first item of business.

If you've never seen good minutes, start from a real [meeting minutes sample](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-minutes-sample) for team and board meetings, then adapt it to your bylaws.

## Should you record board meetings?

This is a genuine judgment call, and boards disagree. A recording makes drafting accurate minutes much easier. It also creates a verbatim record that may be discoverable, and some directors speak less freely when recorded.

Common approaches: record only to assist drafting and delete the recording once minutes are approved; record but keep it with counsel; or don't record at all and rely on a skilled secretary. Whatever you choose, decide it as a board, write the policy down, and tell everyone at the start of each meeting.

## This week's takeaway

Spend less time polishing the board deck and more time on the minutes. The deck persuades the board for an hour. The minutes speak for the board for years.
