Minutes of meeting are the one piece of writing nobody volunteers for and everybody needs. Notes are a convenience. Minutes are a record, and in some meetings they are a legal one.

That difference is why writing them by hand is a bad idea for longer than it seems. The task is not "remember the meeting". It is "produce an accurate record, in a fixed structure, inside a deadline, repeatedly". Software is good at exactly that shape of problem. I switched, and the part that surprised me was not the time saved, it was how much more accurate the records became.

## First: notes and minutes are not the same document

Worth being precise, because conflating them is where most of the pain comes from.

**Notes** are for the people who were there and the ones who missed it. Loose structure, decisions and owners, a page at most. I wrote up the format I use in the companion post linked at the end.

**Minutes** are the official record of a formally constituted meeting: a board, a committee, a general meeting, an association. They carry attendance and quorum, the exact wording of resolutions, who proposed and who seconded, dissent if any, and a signature. The structure is not a style choice.

If your Tuesday team sync is producing "minutes", you are doing paperwork for no reason. If your board meeting is producing "notes", you have a problem.

## The 30-day rule, for Indian companies

This is the detail that catches people, and it is not folklore. Section 118(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 requires minutes of every board, committee and general meeting to be prepared, signed and kept **within 30 days** of the meeting ([Section 118](https://lawx.in/acts/section/24/309/2964)). Section 118(2) requires them to contain a fair and correct summary of the proceedings.

Two consequences people underestimate:

**The clock runs from the meeting, not from when someone gets around to it.** A quarter where the board met in the first week and the minutes were drafted "after results" has already blown the deadline.

**"Fair and correct summary" is not the same as a transcript.** More words are not safer. A long, loose write-up with the resolution text paraphrased is worse than a short one with the resolution quoted exactly.

Secretarial Standard 1, issued by the ICSI, adds the practical timetable most company secretaries actually work to: circulate the draft, collect comments, enter the signed version in the minutes book. If you are responsible for board minutes in India, that standard and the section are the two documents to read, and I would not take a blog post's word for either, including this one.

## What a minutes template has to contain

Independent of jurisdiction, the skeleton is stable:

```
[COMPANY / BODY NAME]
Minutes of the [nth] meeting of the [Board / Committee / Members]
Held on [date] at [time] at [place / by video conference]

PRESENT
  [Name, designation]            Chairperson
  [Name, designation]
IN ATTENDANCE
  [Name, role: company secretary, auditor, invitee]
LEAVE OF ABSENCE
  [Name]

1. CHAIR AND QUORUM
   [Who chaired. That the requisite quorum was present.]

2. [ITEM TITLE]
   [What was placed before the meeting, and by whom.]
   RESOLVED THAT [exact resolution text].
   [Proposed by / seconded by, if your rules require it.]
   [Dissent, recorded by name, if any.]

3. [NEXT ITEM]
   ...

CLOSE
  The meeting concluded at [time].

Signed:  ______________________    Date: __________
         [Name], Chairperson
```

Note what is missing: there is no DISCUSSION block the way there is in meeting notes. Minutes record what was placed, decided and dissented from. Argument that did not change the outcome mostly does not belong, and in a dispute, loose narration of the debate is the part that gets used against you. A ready-made version of this structure with the fields filled in sits in this [meeting minutes sample](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-minutes-sample) if you would rather start from a document than a code block.

## The workflow that replaced the typing

Four steps. The point of each is to move effort before the meeting, where it is cheap, instead of after, where it is expensive.

**1. Build the agenda as the skeleton of the minutes.** Item numbers, titles, and the draft resolution text written out in advance, with blanks for what the meeting decides. Most resolutions are known in advance, because the agenda proposes them. Writing them during the meeting is self-inflicted work.

**2. Record, with everyone's knowledge.** Say it at the start, every time, and for a formal meeting put consent in the record. This is not optional politeness: it is the difference between a useful recording and an inadmissible one, and the rules vary by state and country.

**3. Let software produce the first draft.** This is what changed the job for me. A transcript plus an automatic pass for decisions and action items gets you a draft in minutes rather than an evening, and crucially it gets the numbers and names right, because it is reading rather than remembering. For mixed-language meetings this is where it either works or falls over: plenty of tools transcribe English well and collapse the moment a sentence starts in Hindi and finishes in English. Tools built for [Hinglish](https://lynkk.ai/features/hinglish-transcription), including the one I work on, handle the mid-sentence switch specifically, which matters a lot in Indian boardrooms.

**4. Edit for the record, then sign.** The draft is raw material. Quote the resolutions exactly, cut the narration, confirm attendance and quorum, check every figure against the papers rather than the transcript. This step is yours and cannot be delegated to a tool. It takes me ten to fifteen minutes on a typical board meeting, against the two hours the old way took, and the output is better because I am checking rather than reconstructing.

## What to be careful about

Having run this for a while, three warnings.

**A generated draft is confident about things it has got wrong.** Names, numbers and amounts are where automatic transcription fails, and they are exactly the parts of minutes that matter. Verify them against the agenda papers every time.

**Do not let the tool write the resolution.** Resolutions have settled legal wording. Paste the text from the agenda or the resolution register. A paraphrase that reads fine is still a changed resolution.

**Mind where the recording lives.** Board material is sensitive, and if your organisation has rules about where data may be processed, that question applies to your meeting tool as much as to your document store. Ask where the audio and transcript are stored and who can reach them before you put a board meeting through anything.

## Honest summary

Software did not make minutes easy. It moved the work from typing to checking, which is a much better trade: typing takes hours and introduces errors, checking takes minutes and removes them. The deadline stopped being a problem because the draft now exists the same day, which is the real reason I would not go back.

Start with the structure above and your own agenda. Automate the draft after that, not before, because a generated document with no structure to aim at is just a long transcript, and you cannot sign one of those.
