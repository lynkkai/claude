# The One-on-One Is Where Managers Lose the Thread

*Your report remembers exactly what they told you last time. Do you?*

Welcome back to **After the Call**. This week is for managers, especially new ones, and especially anyone managing more than five people.

## The asymmetry nobody talks about

A one-on-one has two people in it, and they experience it very differently.

For your report, it's *the* meeting. It's the one time each week or fortnight where they get your full attention. They think about it in advance. They remember what they raised.

For you, it's one of seven one-on-ones this week, wedged between a planning meeting and a customer call. You walk in having forgotten that last time they mentioned feeling stretched, or that you promised to look into their training budget.

They notice. Not every time, but often enough. And each time, they learn a little lesson: *what I say here doesn't stick.*

## What gets lost

The things that slip between one-on-ones are rarely the big topics. They're the small signals:

- "I'm fine, just a bit tired with the launch."
- "I'd love to do more customer-facing work at some point."
- "The handoff with design has been a bit rough."
- Your own promise: "Let me check whether we can fund that course."

Each one is a thread. Pick it up next time and you build trust. Drop it and you lose a little.

## A note-taking habit that takes three minutes

You don't need a system. You need three minutes, twice:

**After the one-on-one (90 seconds):**
- What did they raise?
- What did I commit to?
- Anything I should watch for?

**Before the next one (90 seconds):**
- Reread the notes.
- Check your commitments. Did you do them?
- Pick one thing from last time to ask about first.

Opening with "Last time you mentioned the handoff with design was rough. How's that been?" is one of the most powerful sentences a manager can say. It costs nothing and it tells your report you were listening.

## Should you record one-on-ones?

This is sensitive, and the honest answer is: *only if your report genuinely wants it.*

Some people love having a record. They can revisit feedback, and it removes any doubt about what was agreed. Others find a recording inhibiting, especially for conversations about stress, career worries or conflict with colleagues. A one-on-one where your report self-censors is worse than one with no notes at all.

If you do record:

- ask first, every time, and make "no" an easy answer;
- keep the notes private between the two of you;
- never use a recording of a one-on-one in a performance process without explicit agreement.

For managers who want help remembering without the weight of a recording, the three-minute written habit above is enough. For those whose reports prefer a record, tools exist that keep [one-on-one notes](https://lynkk.ai/use-cases/one-on-one-meetings) private to the people involved.

## The compound effect

Here's what happens after a few months of this habit. Your one-on-ones start with continuity instead of catch-up. Your reports bring better topics, because they trust they'll be followed up. Small problems surface earlier, because people mention them when they're still small.

None of it requires a framework. It requires remembering, and remembering requires writing things down. The same principle applies to any recurring meeting; there's a short [meeting preparation checklist](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-preparation) that generalizes it.

## This week's takeaway

Before your next one-on-one, spend 90 seconds rereading what your report said last time. Then ask about it first.
