# The Five Minutes Before a Meeting Decide Everything

*This story was written with AI assistance and edited by the Lynkk team.*

Think about the last meeting that went badly. Not catastrophically, just badly. You spent the first ten minutes re-establishing what you'd covered last time. Someone asked about a promise you'd forgotten making. You left without the decision you needed.

Now think about why. Usually it wasn't the agenda. It was that you walked in cold.

## Walking in cold is the default

In a back-to-back day, the gap between meetings is the time it takes to click the next link. There's no room to remember what happened last time with this person or this customer, so you rebuild it live, in front of them.

Most meeting advice focuses on the agenda: send it in advance, keep it short, put decisions first. That's good advice. But an agenda tells you what you're going to talk about. It doesn't tell you what you already know.

## The four questions worth five minutes

Before any meeting that matters (a customer call, a one-on-one, a board prep), it's worth answering four questions. They take about five minutes if your notes are findable and about thirty if they aren't.

**1. What did we decide last time?**
Decisions are the backbone of continuity. If you agreed to a pilot with 15 users, you don't want to re-litigate it.

**2. What did I promise?**
This is the question that saves reputations. "I'll send the security overview by Friday." Did you?

**3. What's still open?**
Unresolved questions from last time are usually the most important items for this time.

**4. What did they say that I might have missed?**
Customers, direct reports and investors say things in passing that turn out to matter: a new stakeholder, a budget change, a frustration. Rereading their exact words often changes your plan.

There's a simple [meeting preparation checklist and brief template](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-preparation) on the Lynkk blog built around these questions.

## Why this is hard without good notes

The four questions are easy to answer if you have a short, structured record of every past meeting with that person or account. They're almost impossible if your record is a mix of half-finished notes, three different recording folders and memory.

That's the real argument for capturing meetings well. Not the archive itself, but the five minutes before the next meeting.

## Where AI assistants actually help

There's a lot of noise about AI meeting assistants. Most of them summarize a meeting after it happens. That's useful, but it's only one of three moments where help matters:

- **Before:** surfacing what was discussed, promised and left open in past meetings with the same people.
- **During:** answering a question in the moment ("what did they say about budget last month?") without leaving the call.
- **After:** summaries, action items with owners, and follow-ups.

A tool that only helps *after* still leaves you walking in cold. When you compare tools, ask what each one does before and during the call, not just after. Our team looked at [8 AI meeting assistants](https://lynkk.ai/blog/ai-powered-meeting-assistants) through exactly that lens.

## The one-on-one is the clearest case

Nowhere does preparation matter more than in a one-on-one. Your direct report remembers exactly what they raised last time. If you don't, they notice.

A two-minute review before each one-on-one (what they raised, what you committed to, what's still open) changes the tone of the conversation. It tells them you were listening. If you run regular 1:1s, here are [20 one-on-one meeting questions and a notes template](https://lynkk.ai/blog/one-on-one-meeting) that make this review easy.

## Build the habit in one week

1. **Block five minutes** before your three most important meetings this week. Literally put it in the calendar.
2. **Answer the four questions** for each one, in writing, even if it's three bullet points.
3. **After each meeting**, note one thing the prep changed: a promise you remembered, a topic you raised, a mistake you avoided.

At the end of the week you'll know whether the five minutes earned their place. If they did, keep the block. If they didn't, you've lost fifteen minutes and learned something about your meetings.
