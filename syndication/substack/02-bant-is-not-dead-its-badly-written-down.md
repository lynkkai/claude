# BANT Isn't Dead. It's Just Badly Written Down.

*The world's most criticized qualification framework fails less in the call than in the notes afterwards.*

Welcome back to **After the Call**. This week: a sales framework that's been declared dead more times than we can count, and why the funeral might be premature.

## The case against BANT

BANT stands for **Budget, Authority, Need and Timeline**. IBM popularized it decades ago, and every few years someone writes a post explaining why it no longer works. The arguments are familiar:

- Buyers don't know their budget early on.
- "Authority" is now a committee, not a person.
- Asking "do you have budget?" in the first call feels like an interrogation.
- It's seller-centered rather than buyer-centered.

All fair. But notice what these criticisms are about: **how the questions are asked.** Almost none of them say the four things BANT tracks don't matter. Budget, decision-makers, need and timing still decide whether deals close.

## Where BANT really fails

Here's a pattern that's easy to recognize once you look for it: BANT often breaks down after the call, not during it.

A rep asks good questions. The customer gives a nuanced answer: *"We've set aside something like thirty for this, but honestly if the pilot goes well, finance said they'd look at more."* The rep writes "Budget: ✓" in the CRM.

Three weeks later, the deal stalls on price, and nobody remembers that the budget was conditional on the pilot.

That's the real BANT failure mode: **compressing rich answers into checkboxes.** "Authority: yes" hides the fact that IT has a veto. "Timeline: Q1" hides that Q1 depends on a reorganization. The framework asked the right questions. The notes threw away the answers.

## Writing BANT down properly

Here's a simple rule that fixes most of it: **for each letter, write down the customer's words, not your verdict.**

| Letter | Checkbox version | Better version |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | ✓ | "Around $30K set aside; more possible if pilot succeeds (Priya, discovery call)" |
| Authority | VP Eng | "Priya owns evaluation; IT must sign off security review before rollout" |
| Need | Context loss | "Reps rebuild each account from scratch between calls" |
| Timeline | Q1 | "Pilot after IT review; no hard date yet" |

The better version takes 30 extra seconds and saves the deal review a week later. It also tells your manager what you actually know versus what you're assuming.

If you want the full breakdown (what each letter means, the questions that get honest answers, and where BANT really does fall short), we wrote a detailed guide to [BANT sales qualification](https://lynkk.ai/blog/bant-sales-qualification).

## Let the recording do the remembering

The reason reps compress answers into checkboxes is simple: they're writing from memory, after the call, between two other calls. Memory compresses automatically.

Recording the call (with the customer's consent) changes that. The exact words are captured. A good note format then pulls budget, stakeholders, pain points and next steps into separate sections, each tied to the moment it was said. The rep's job becomes checking the notes, not writing them.

That's the workflow behind most modern [sales call notetakers](https://lynkk.ai/use-cases/sales-calls), including ours.

## BANT is best used before the next call

The most underrated use of BANT notes isn't qualification. It's preparation.

Before your second call with a prospect, read your BANT notes and ask: what don't we know yet? If Authority says "IT must sign off," your next call should include a question about the IT review. If Budget says "more possible if pilot succeeds," you know what the pilot has to prove.

Five minutes of prep with good notes beats thirty minutes of rapport-building with bad ones. We wrote up a short [meeting preparation checklist](https://lynkk.ai/blog/meeting-preparation) built around this idea.

## This week's takeaway

BANT doesn't fail because the letters are wrong. It fails because the answers get reduced to ticks. Write down the customer's words, and the old framework starts working again.
