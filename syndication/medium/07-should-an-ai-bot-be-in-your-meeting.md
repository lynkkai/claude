# Should an AI Bot Be in Your Meeting? An Honest Look at Bots vs Bot-Free Note Takers

*This story was written with AI assistance and edited by the Lynkk team.*

You've seen it. You join a call and there's an extra tile in the grid: "Alex's Notetaker." Nobody says anything about it. Somebody turns their camera off.

AI note takers have become normal fast, and the most visible design choice they make is whether to send a bot into the meeting or record quietly from your own computer. Both approaches have real strengths. Neither is right for every meeting.

## How bot note takers work

A bot note taker joins the call as a participant. It usually connects to your calendar, joins meetings with a Zoom, Google Meet or Teams link when they start, records the audio (sometimes video), and leaves when the call ends. You get a transcript and summary afterwards.

**What bots do well:**

- **They work without your device.** You can send a bot to a call you can't attend.
- **Everyone can see them.** The bot is in the participant list, which is a form of disclosure. Nobody can claim they didn't know a recording tool was present.
- **Speaker names are easy.** Because the bot is inside the meeting, it can often label speakers by their real participant names.
- **They're platform-agnostic.** One bot can join Zoom, Meet and Teams.

**Where bots struggle:**

- **They change the room.** Some people talk less freely with a visible recorder. Some external guests remove the bot, or ask for it to leave.
- **Hosts can block them.** Waiting rooms, lobby settings and admin policies can keep bots out.
- **They don't work in person.** A bot can't join a conference room conversation.

If you're curious about the mechanics, here's how to [add a meeting bot to a Zoom or Google Meet call](https://lynkk.ai/blog/zoom-bot-join-meeting), including what to do when the bot can't get past the waiting room.

## How bot-free note takers work

A bot-free note taker records on your own machine instead. A desktop app or browser extension captures your microphone and the meeting's audio, then transcribes it. There's no extra participant in the call.

**What bot-free capture does well:**

- **No change to the room.** The meeting looks exactly as it did before.
- **Works with any app on your computer**, including ones bots can't join.
- **Works in person.** The same app can record a conversation around a table.

**Where it struggles:**

- **You have to be there.** Your device does the recording.
- **Disclosure is on you.** Because nothing in the call signals that it's being recorded, you must say so yourself. Every time.
- **Speaker labels are harder.** The app knows which voice is yours (your microphone) but has to tell other speakers apart by voice.

## The consent question is the same either way

Here is the part that matters most, and it doesn't depend on the tool.

Recording laws differ by country and by state. Some places require only one person's consent, others require everyone's. A visible bot is not consent. A bot-free recorder is not a loophole. **Say at the start of the call that you're recording, and why.** It takes five seconds and removes almost every awkward moment later.

Any tool marketed as a way to record people "without them noticing" is a tool to avoid.

## Which should you use?

A rough guide:

| Situation | Better fit |
|---|---|
| Internal team meetings you attend | Either; bot-free is lighter |
| Calls you can't attend but need notes from | Bot |
| Sensitive external calls where a visible bot feels heavy | Bot-free, with verbal disclosure |
| Meetings where hosts block bots | Bot-free |
| In-person meetings and interviews | Bot-free |
| Large webinars | Bot, or the platform's own recording |

Many teams end up using both: a bot for routine calls and calls they miss, and a desktop app for everything else. Lynkk, for example, offers a meeting bot for Zoom, Meet and Teams alongside a Mac app and Chrome extension that record without one, and a browser recorder for meetings in the room.

## Platform matters too

Your meeting platform shapes the choice. Zoom has its own AI Companion on paid plans, and Teams has Copilot features for licensed users. These are often the simplest option if you meet on one platform only. They get weaker when you work across several.

If you're on Zoom, there's a comparison of [Zoom note takers](https://lynkk.ai/blog/zoom-note-taker), from AI Companion to bot-free apps. If you're on Teams, here's an overview of [AI note takers for Microsoft Teams](https://lynkk.ai/blog/ai-note-taker-microsoft-teams) and when Copilot is or isn't worth it.

## The short version

Bots are best when you need notes from meetings you can't be in, and when visible disclosure helps. Bot-free is best when you're in the room (virtual or physical) and want the meeting to feel like a meeting. Either way, tell people you're recording. That part isn't a feature; it's manners.
