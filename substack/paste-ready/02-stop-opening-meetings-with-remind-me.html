There's a sentence that quietly tells the other side of the call you didn't prepare.

"So, remind me where we landed last time?"

It sounds friendly. It costs more than it looks. The customer now has to do your homework, out loud, in front of their colleagues. And their version of "where we landed" is rarely the same as yours.

This post is a fix you can use on your very next call: a five-minute brief, a three-line pre-read, and one end-of-meeting habit that makes the next brief almost free.

Why nobody preps (and why that's rational)

Telling people to "prepare better for meetings" is like telling them to drink more water. True, useless.

The calendar doesn't allow it. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found that 57% of meetings are ad hoc calls with no invite, half of all meetings land in people's peak focus hours, and the average worker is interrupted about every two minutes during the core day.

And every interruption has a long tail. Gloria Mark's research at UC Irvine found it took people about 23 minutes on average to get back to an interrupted task. Carving 20 minutes of prep out of that day isn't discipline. It's fantasy.

So the goal isn't "more prep." It's prep that fits in five minutes, and prep that mostly already exists.

The five-question brief

Before any call with someone you've met before, answer these five questions. Write the answers down, even if it's three words each. Writing forces you to notice what you don't know.

1. What did we decide last time?

Decisions, not discussion. "They're going with the annual plan" is a decision. "We talked about pricing" is not. If you can't find a decision, that's your first agenda item.

2. What did each side promise, and did it happen?

List every "I'll send that over" from both sides. Check whether yours went out. If theirs didn't, you now know how to open the call without sounding like a debt collector: "Last time you mentioned looping in your security team. How did that go?"

3. What changed since?

New emails, support tickets, a new person cc'd, a funding announcement, a reorg. The meeting is happening in the present, not in the transcript of five weeks ago.

4. What's the one outcome for this call?

One. Not three. If the call ends and only one thing happened, what should it be? A date for the pilot, a yes on scope, a named decision-maker.

5. Who's in the room, and what do they care about?

Anyone new on the invite changes the meeting. A new finance person means a cost conversation is coming whether you planned one or not.

If you can't answer question 1, you're not ready for the call. You're ready for a recap.

Send a three-line pre-read

Your brief makes you warm. A pre-read makes the whole room warm, and it's the single most underrated move in customer-facing work.

Send this a few hours before the call (or the night before):

Hi [name], looking forward to [day/time].

Where we left off: [the decision from Q1]
Open items: [your promise, status] / [their promise, status]
Goal for this call: [the one outcome from Q4]

Anything you'd add?

Three things happen. People show up with the same starting point. Missing items surface before the call instead of during it. And "anything you'd add?" often gets you the real agenda, the thing they were planning to bring up anyway.

The habit that makes the next brief write itself

Here's the trick: the best time to write the brief for the next meeting is the last two minutes of this one.

Before you hang up, say out loud:

"Let me play back what I heard: we decided X, I'm sending Y by Thursday, you're checking Z with your team. Did I miss anything?"

Then paste that playback into your notes, exactly as said. That paragraph is questions 1 and 2 of your next brief. Next time, you only need to answer 3, 4 and 5, which takes about two minutes.

It also catches misunderstandings while everyone is still in the room, which is the cheapest place to catch them.
